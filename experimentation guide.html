S1

Experimentation Fundamentals

Quick ref

The vocabulary and core logic behind every controlled experiment. Get the units right and most downstream statistics take care of themselves.

Why experimentation mattersREF

Experiments are the only scalable way to attribute a change in outcomes to a change in the product. At Microsoft, Booking.com and Airbnb, only ~10–30% of ideas tested actually improve key metrics — intuition alone systematically ships the other 70–90%.

Business exampleA low-priority Bing idea (moving ad title lines) sat in the backlog for months; when finally tested it produced one of the largest revenue lifts in Bing's history. Nobody could rank ideas by opinion.
Key takeawayOpinions don't scale; experiments do. The value of an experimentation culture is mostly in the ideas it stops you from shipping.
RandomizationREF

Random assignment balances observed and unobserved user characteristics across groups in expectation. Any remaining outcome difference is attributable to the treatment plus quantifiable sampling noise — this is what licenses causal language.

Business exampleIn practice: bucket = hash(user_id + experiment_salt) % 100. Deterministic, stable across sessions, independent across experiments via the salt.
Key takeawayRandomization replaces "control for everything" with "balance everything by design." When it breaks (SRM), nothing downstream can be trusted.
Treatment vs ControlREF

The control group is your live counterfactual: what would have happened without the change. It must run concurrently (never compare to last month) and remain untouched by the treatment experience.

Business exampleNetflix tests new artwork: control sees existing artwork during the same weeks, absorbing the same seasonality, releases, and outages as treatment.
Key takeawayConcurrent control absorbs time effects for free. Most "before vs after" wins are seasonality wearing a costume.
Experiment UnitsREF

The unit you randomize: user, device, session, listing, geo, or time window. Choose the unit where (a) interference between units is minimal and (b) the unit matches the decision you'll make.

Business exampleUber can't randomize riders for a pricing change — riders share one driver pool — so it randomizes city×time windows (switchbacks) instead.
Key takeawayUnit choice is a causal decision, not a plumbing decision. Wrong unit → biased estimate, no statistics can rescue it.
Exposure UnitsREF

The subset of randomized units that could actually encounter the change. If only 5% of assigned users reach the changed screen, the other 95% contribute pure noise and dilute the measured effect ~20×.

Business exampleA checkout redesign at Expedia: randomize all visitors, but only those who reach checkout are exposed. Triggered analysis restricts to them (see S5).
Key takeawayMeasured lift ≈ true lift on exposed × exposure rate. Always know your trigger rate before judging an effect size.
Analysis UnitsREF

The unit your metric is computed over. When analysis unit ≠ randomization unit (randomize users, analyze sessions or page-views), observations are correlated within users and naive variance formulas are wrong — usually too small.

Business exampleSession conversion with user-level randomization: heavy users contribute many correlated sessions. Use the delta method or user-level bootstrap for correct CIs.
Key takeawayIf analysis unit is finer than randomization unit, your p-values are optimistic unless you correct for clustering.
Hypothesis DesignREF

A testable hypothesis names the change, the population, the metric, the expected direction and size, and the mechanism: "If we [change X] for [population], [metric] will move by [≥ MDE] because [mechanism]."

Business example"If we show total price upfront to EU guests, booking conversion drops ≤0.5% while customer-service contacts about fees fall ≥10%, because pricing surprise at checkout is removed."
Key takeawayThe mechanism clause is what makes a result interpretable. No mechanism → no learning, even from a win.
Success MetricsREF

Pick one primary metric before launch: sensitive enough to move in weeks, attributable to the change, and directionally aligned with long-term value. Surround it with guardrails (must-not-harm) and diagnostics (explain the movement).

Business exampleAirbnb search experiments: primary = search→booking conversion; guardrails = cancellation rate, host-side response rate; diagnostics = result CTR, position of booked listing.
Key takeawayOne pre-registered primary metric. Everything chosen after seeing the data is a hypothesis for the next experiment.
S2

Statistical Foundations

Quick ref

Twelve concepts that cover ~90% of the statistics used in product experimentation. The error-rate diagram at the end ties them together.

ProbabilityREF

The long-run frequency of an event (frequentist) or a degree of belief (Bayesian). Every product metric — conversion, retention, CTR — is a sample estimate of an underlying probability or expectation.

Business example"Conversion is 2.3%" means: of sessions observed, 2.3% booked. The true P(book) is unknown; your data estimates it with noise.
Key takeawayDashboards show estimates, not truths. The whole testing apparatus exists to quantify how wrong the estimate could be.
Conditional ProbabilityREF

P(A|B) — the probability of A among units where B holds. Comparing P(outcome | feature users) to P(outcome | non-users) is the most common analysis pattern, and the most commonly misread one: who ends up in B is rarely random.

Business exampleP(rebook | used wishlist) = 40% vs 12% overall. Wishlist users were high-intent before they touched the feature — conditioning ≠ causation.
Key takeawayEvery "users who do X retain better" insight is conditional probability. Treat it as a hypothesis to test, never a result to ship.
Bayes' TheoremREF

P(H|data) ∝ P(data|H) · P(H). Updates a prior belief with evidence. Its sharpest product application: interpreting a "significant" result given how often ideas truly work.

Business exampleIf only 10% of ideas have real effects, α=.05, power=.80: P(real | significant) = (.10×.80)/(.10×.80 + .90×.05) ≈ 64%. A third of your "wins" are false.
Key takeawaySignificance is not certainty. Low-prior, surprising wins deserve replication before a big rollout.
Expected ValueREF

The probability-weighted average of outcomes: E[X] = Σ p(x)·x. Nearly every primary metric (revenue per user, bookings per visitor) is a sample mean estimating an expected value.

Business exampleRevenue/visitor = P(purchase) × E[order value | purchase]. Decomposing an EV like this tells you which lever an experiment actually moved.
Key takeawayDecompose mean metrics into rate × value. Treatments often move one component while the headline metric hides it.
VarianceREF

Average squared deviation from the mean — the noise floor of your experiment. Heavy-tailed metrics (revenue, GMV) have enormous variance; a few whales can dominate the estimate.

Business exampleBooking value at Expedia spans $40 hostels to $40k villas. Same sample size detects a 1% conversion lift easily but a 1% revenue lift not at all.
Key takeawayVariance — not the mean — decides how long experiments take. This is why variance reduction (S5) is a whole discipline.
Central Limit TheoremREF

Means of large independent samples are approximately normal, regardless of the metric's own distribution. This is why z/t-tests work on skewed metrics like revenue — the mean is well-behaved even when the data isn't.

CLT · skewed data → normal sampling distribution of the mean
CaveatCLT assumes independent units. Ratio metrics and clustered data (sessions within users) need the delta method or bootstrap.
Confidence IntervalsREF

A 95% CI is a procedure that captures the true effect in 95% of repeated experiments. Width = precision; the point estimate is just the center of your uncertainty.

Business exampleLift +1.2%, CI [−0.1%, +2.5%]: includes zero → not significant, but also consistent with a +2% win. The right read is "underpowered," not "no effect."
Key takeawayReport CIs, not just stars. A CI of [+0.1%, +0.3%] and [+0.1%, +8%] are both "significant" and mean completely different things.
Hypothesis TestingREF

Assume no effect (H₀), then measure how surprising the observed difference would be under that assumption. If sufficiently surprising (p < α), reject H₀. Default to two-sided tests — regressions matter as much as wins.

Key takeawayA test controls how often you fool yourself, nothing more. It says nothing about effect size or business value — pair it with the CI and a practical-significance threshold.
P-valuesREF

P(data at least this extreme | H₀ is true). It is not P(H₀ | data), not the probability the result is a fluke, and not a measure of effect size.

Business examplep=0.03 with n=10M can mean a +0.05% lift nobody should ship; p=0.08 with n=5k can hide a +5% win worth re-running with more traffic.
Key takeawayp answers "how surprising is this data under no-effect?" — combine with prior win rates (Bayes) and CI width before deciding.
Type I Error (α)REF

False positive: declaring an effect that doesn't exist. Rate set by α (typically 5%). Cost: shipping noise, polluting the roadmap with "wins" that never replicate, eroding trust in the platform.

Key takeawayα is a budget you spend per look and per metric. Peeking and multiple testing (S3) silently overspend it.
Type II Error (β)REF

False negative: missing a real effect. Rate β depends on sample size, effect size, variance, and α. Cost: killing good ideas — usually invisible, because nobody audits the experiments that "didn't work."

Key takeawayUnderpowered tests don't just miss effects — the wins they do find are systematically exaggerated (winner's curse).
Statistical Power (1−β)REF

Probability of detecting an effect of a given size if it's real. Industry default: ≥80% at the MDE. The diagram shows how α, β, and power partition the two sampling distributions.

H₀ vs H₁ sampling distributions · α, β, power
Key takeawayPower is decided before launch via sample size (S4). "We'll just run it longer if it's close" is peeking with extra steps.

A/B Testing

Quick ref

Experiment lifecycle

2 · HypothesisChange → metric → direction → size → mechanismNo mechanism, no MDE
3 · MetricsPre-register primary, guardrails, diagnosticsMetric shopping after results
4 · LaunchPower calc, ramp plan, randomization checkUnderpowered, no SRM check
5 · MonitoringWatch guardrails + SRM, not significancePeeking at the p-value daily

Metric hierarchy

Metric tree · north star → drivers, fenced by guardrails
North Star

Long-term value proxy: nights booked (Airbnb), hours watched (Netflix), orders delivered (DoorDash). Too slow to be a test's primary metric — experiments move its drivers.

Guardrails

Must-not-harm: latency, crash rate, cancellations, unsubscribes, support contacts. Tested for non-inferiority, with a pre-agreed tolerable regression.

Diagnostics

Explain why the primary moved: funnel steps, CTR, error rates. Never used for ship decisions — only for understanding and debugging.

Common pitfalls

Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM)CRITICAL

The observed assignment split deviates from design more than chance allows (chi-square test). At 1M users, even 50.2/49.8 is a giant red flag. Causes: bot filtering applied unevenly, redirects dropping one arm, logging loss, triggering bugs.

RuleSRM means the missing users are non-random — discard the result entirely. Microsoft found ~6% of experiments hit SRM; debugging it is step zero of every analysis.
PeekingPITFALL

Checking significance repeatedly and stopping on the first p < .05. Daily peeks over a few weeks can push the real false-positive rate from 5% toward 20–30% — extreme values occur by chance mid-flight and you harvest them.

FixCommit to a fixed horizon, or use sequential methods built for continuous monitoring (S5). Monitoring guardrails for harm is fine; harvesting wins early is not.
Novelty & Primacy EffectsPITFALL

Novelty: users poke at anything new, inflating early lift that decays. Primacy: users trained on the old experience underperform at first, masking a true win. Both make week-1 reads misleading.

FixPlot effect by days-since-first-exposure; compare new vs returning users; run ≥2 full business cycles; use long-term holdouts (S7) for the durable effect.
Multiple TestingPITFALL

20 metrics × 10 segments = 200 tests; at α=.05 you expect ~10 "significant" results from pure noise. Segment fishing is the polite name for it.

FixOne pre-registered primary; Benjamini–Hochberg correction for secondary metrics; treat any surprising segment result as a hypothesis for a follow-up test, never a finding.
Survivorship BiasPITFALL

Analyzing only users who stuck around ("retained users in treatment spend more") conditions on a post-treatment outcome. If treatment changed who survives, the comparison is broken.

FixIntention-to-treat: analyze all randomized units in their assigned groups, including users who churned, bounced, or never engaged.

S4

Sample Size & Power

Formulas

Three views of one equation. Fix any three of {n, MDE, power, α} and the fourth is determined. Knowledge-book mode: formulas and interpretation, no calculators.

Sample size

n per arm = 2 · (z1−α/2 + z1−β)² · σ² / δ²  // δ = MDE (absolute), σ² = metric variance
proportions: σ² ≈ p̄(1−p̄)  // p̄ = pooled baseline rate
rule of thumb (α=.05, power=.80): n ≈ 16 · σ² / δ²
Reading the formulaConsequence
δ² in the denominatorHalve the MDE → 4× the sample. Detecting subtle effects is quadratically expensive.
σ² in the numeratorCut variance 30% (CUPED, S5) → run 30% fewer users for the same power.
z-terms grow with stricter α / higher powerα=.01 or power=.90 each add ~30–50% more sample.
Worked exampleBaseline 10% conversion, MDE +0.5pp absolute: n ≈ 16 × .09 / .0005² ≈ 57,600 per arm.

Minimum Detectable Effect

MDE = (z1−α/2 + z1−β) · σ · √(2/n)  // smallest true effect you'd detect with the planned power

Use it in reverse: given the traffic you actually have, what's detectable? If your realistic MDE is +3% but plausible wins are +0.5%, the experiment cannot answer the question — change the metric, reduce variance, or don't run it.

InterpretationMDE is a property of the design, not a prediction of the effect. "Non-significant" only means "no effect ≥ MDE was detected."

Statistical power

power = 1 − β = P(reject H₀ | true effect = δ)  // convention: ≥ 0.80 at the MDE
LeverEffect on powerCost
More units (n↑)↑Calendar time, traffic share
Bigger true effect (δ↑)↑Not controllable — but bolder variants help
Lower variance (σ↓)↑CUPED/stratification engineering (S5)
Looser α↑More false positives
One-sided test↑Blind to regressions — rarely justified
S5

Advanced Experimentation

Deep dive

Variance reduction and sensitivity techniques — how mature platforms (Microsoft EXP, Netflix XP, DoorDash Curie) detect smaller effects with the same traffic.

CUPEDDEEP

Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data. Adjust each unit's outcome using its own pre-experiment value of (usually) the same metric. Because pre-period behavior predicts post-period behavior but cannot be affected by treatment, subtracting the predictable part removes noise without touching the treatment effect.

Yadj = Y − θ·(X − E[X])  // X = pre-period covariate
θ = cov(X,Y) / var(X)    variance reduction = ρ²  // ρ = corr(X,Y)

If pre/post correlation ρ = 0.7, variance drops ~49% — roughly halving required sample size or runtime. Microsoft reported ~50% runtime reductions on engagement metrics after adopting it platform-wide.

Assumptions
  • Covariate measured strictly pre-assignment (unaffected by treatment)
  • Covariate correlates with the outcome
  • Same θ applied to both arms
Watch-outs
  • New users have no pre-period → adjust only matched users, or set X to the mean
  • Weak correlation → negligible gain (it never hurts in expectation)
  • Doesn't fix bias — only shrinks variance
ExampleNetflix tests a new row-ranking model on hours watched. Last month's hours per user explains most of this month's variance; CUPED on it detects a 0.3% lift that a raw t-test would call noise.
CUPACDEEP

Control Using Predictions As Covariates (DoorDash). Same algebra as CUPED, but the covariate is an ML model's prediction of the outcome built from many pre-experiment features. The better the model, the higher ρ, the bigger the variance cut.

When it beats CUPED
  • No single strong pre-period metric exists (new surfaces, sparse users)
  • Many weak signals combine into a strong predictor
  • Delivery/logistics metrics driven by context (region, daypart, weather)
Watch-outs
  • Model must train only on pre-experiment / outside-experiment data
  • Leakage of treatment info into features biases the estimate
  • Adds MLOps cost — justify with measured sensitivity gains
ExampleDoorDash predicts delivery time from store, region, and historical load, then uses the prediction as the CUPED covariate for switchback experiments — cutting required experiment duration materially.
Covariate Adjustment (Regression)DEEP

Regress the outcome on the treatment indicator plus pre-treatment covariates (platform, country, tenure, pre-activity). The treatment coefficient is the adjusted effect; precision improves in proportion to how much the covariates explain.

Pros
  • Handles many covariates at once; standard tooling
  • Equivalent to CUPED with one covariate; generalizes it
  • Also corrects chance imbalance on observables
Cons
  • Never include post-treatment variables — conditioning on outcomes of the treatment (e.g., sessions after exposure) breaks randomization
  • Inference should use robust (HC) standard errors
  • Temptation to tweak the model after seeing results
Stratified RandomizationDEEP

Split units into strata (country, platform, user tier) and randomize within each, guaranteeing balance on those variables instead of hoping for it. Variance shrinks by the between-strata share of total variance.

Pros
  • Exact balance on chosen strata even in small samples
  • Pre-planned subgroup reads are valid and powered
  • Critical for geo/cluster experiments with few units
Cons
  • Must be wired into the assignment service, not bolted on later
  • Only balances what you stratify on
  • Analysis must respect strata (stratified estimator)
ExampleA geo experiment with 40 markets stratifies on market size and region before assignment — otherwise one unlucky draw puts all mega-cities in treatment.
Triggered AnalysisDEEP

Restrict analysis to units that actually reached the change ("triggered"), removing the diluting mass of never-exposed users. With a 5% trigger rate, a +4% effect on the exposed shows up as +0.2% overall — triggering recovers the sensitivity.

Requirements
  • Counterfactual triggering: control units are flagged at the moment they would have seen the change — requires logging the counterfactual decision
  • Trigger condition must be identical and pre-treatment in both arms
  • Check SRM within the triggered population
Watch-outs
  • Triggered lift ≠ launch impact: dilute back (overall ≈ triggered lift × trigger share of the metric)
  • If treatment changes who triggers, the populations differ → biased
ExampleBooking.com tests a new low-availability banner shown only when ≤3 rooms remain. Analysis runs on sessions where the condition fired, with control sessions flagged counterfactually.
Sequential TestingDEEP

Methods that keep α valid under continuous monitoring: group-sequential boundaries (O'Brien–Fleming alpha-spending) or always-valid inference (mSPRT, used by Optimizely/Netflix-style platforms). Legalizes "peeking" by paying for it upfront.

Pros
  • Stop early on big wins or harmful regressions — ethical & fast
  • Honest error control with daily dashboards
  • Fits ramp-up cultures (ship fast when evidence is strong)
Cons
  • Less power than a fixed-horizon test at the same final n
  • Early stops overestimate effect sizes (condition on extremes)
  • More complex to implement and explain to stakeholders

S6

Marketplace Experimentation

Quick ref

Two-sided marketplaces (Airbnb, Uber, DoorDash) break the core A/B assumption — units share finite supply, so treatment and control contaminate each other.

Two-sided interference · treatment steals inventory from control
Supply metrics

Active listings, new-host activation, calendar availability, acceptance rate, host churn.

Demand metrics

Searchers, search→request rate, booking requests, guest conversion, repeat booking.

Liquidity metrics

Search→book rate, fill/utilization, match rate, time-to-match, % searches with ≥N relevant results.

Marketplace health

Cancellation rate, price dispersion, supply/demand ratio by market, new-host exposure share, take-rate stability.

Network effects & spilloversPITFALL

A guest-side feature that lifts treatment bookings consumes listings that control guests would have booked. Control is degraded, not neutral — the A/B comparison measures (treatment gain + control loss) and overstates the launch effect, sometimes severely. This is cannibalization / SUTVA violation.

ExampleA ranking change that surfaces high-demand Paris listings: treatment books them first, control finds them gone. The test shows +2%; the launch delivers +0.5% because in launch there's no one to steal from.
MitigationsRandomize at the market level (geo experiments), use switchbacks for shared-state levers, randomize the supply side for host features, or model the equilibrium. See S7 and the marketplace framework in S11.
S7

When A/B Testing Is Not Possible

Deep dive

The core skill of a senior experimentation scientist: choosing a credible causal design when clean randomization is off the table. Start with the walker, then read the method cards.

Interactive · When Randomized Experiments Cannot Be Run

Method cards

Difference-in-Differences (DiD)DEEP

Problem solved: a change hits one group/region at a known time while comparable groups remain untreated. DiD compares the treated group's before→after change against the control group's, differencing out both time-invariant group differences and shared time shocks.

effect = (Ytreat,post − Ytreat,pre) − (Yctrl,post − Yctrl,pre)
Assumptions
  • Parallel trends: absent treatment, both groups would have moved identically
  • No spillover from treated to control units
  • Stable group composition; no simultaneous group-specific shocks
Diagnostics
  • Plot long pre-period trends — they should be visually parallel
  • Placebo test: fake treatment date in the pre-period should show ~0
  • Event-study plot of dynamic effects
Pros
  • Simple, transparent, easily explained to leadership
  • Controls for fixed group differences and common shocks
  • Works with aggregate data
Cons
  • Parallel trends is untestable in the post-period
  • Staggered adoption + heterogeneous effects biases the classic two-way FE estimator (use modern estimators, e.g. Callaway–Sant'Anna)
  • Sensitive to control-group choice
Use caseUber changes its service-fee structure in Chicago. Compare Chicago's booking trend before/after vs a set of comparable non-launch cities over the same weeks.
Synthetic ControlDEEP

Problem solved: only one treated unit (a city, a country) and no single control matches it. Build a weighted combination of untreated "donor" units that reproduces the treated unit's pre-period trajectory; the post-period gap is the effect.

Assumptions
  • Donor units untreated and unaffected by spillover
  • Pre-period long enough to fit weights without overfitting
  • No post-period shocks unique to the treated unit
Diagnostics
  • Pre-period fit quality (RMSPE)
  • Placebo-in-space: apply to each donor; treated effect should be extreme in that distribution
  • Leave-one-out donor sensitivity
Pros
  • Data-driven, transparent counterfactual (weights are inspectable)
  • Made for one-unit policy/launch evaluations
Cons
  • Formal inference is awkward (permutation/placebo based)
  • Fails when no donor combination matches the pre-trend
  • Vulnerable to overfitting short pre-periods
Use caseA new short-term-rental regulation hits Amsterdam. Weight Berlin, Copenhagen, Vienna… to mimic Amsterdam's pre-regulation nights-booked curve; the divergence after enforcement estimates the impact.
Regression Discontinuity (RDD)DEEP

Problem solved: treatment assigned by a sharp rule on a running variable (score ≥ cutoff → treated). Units just above and just below the cutoff are as-good-as-randomized, so comparing them near the threshold identifies a local causal effect.

Assumptions
  • Units cannot precisely manipulate their running variable (check density continuity — McCrary test)
  • Potential outcomes continuous at the cutoff
  • Nothing else changes at the same threshold
Diagnostics
  • Density plot around the cutoff (no bunching)
  • Covariate smoothness across the threshold
  • Bandwidth & polynomial sensitivity checks
Pros
  • Near-experimental credibility without randomizing
  • Thresholds are everywhere in products: eligibility scores, tiers, minimums
Cons
  • Estimates a local effect at the cutoff only
  • Needs dense data near the threshold
  • Fuzzy compliance requires an IV-style estimator
Use caseSuperhost status requires a 4.8 rating. Compare hosts at 4.79 vs 4.81 on subsequent bookings to estimate the causal value of the badge.
Instrumental Variables (IV)DEEP

Problem solved: treatment is self-selected (confounded), but some variable — the instrument — shifts treatment uptake while affecting the outcome only through treatment. In product work the cleanest instrument is a randomized encouragement: you can't force feature adoption, but you can randomize a nudge toward it.

effect (LATE) = (effect of instrument on outcome) / (effect of instrument on treatment uptake)
Assumptions
  • Relevance: instrument moves uptake (testable)
  • Exclusion: instrument affects outcome only via treatment (untestable — argue it)
  • Independence: instrument as-good-as-random
  • Monotonicity: no defiers
Diagnostics
  • First-stage F-stat (weak instrument if small)
  • Balance of covariates across instrument values
  • Reduced-form effect sanity check
Pros
  • Handles unobserved confounding — PSM cannot
  • Encouragement designs are cheap to run on top of normal A/B infra
Cons
  • Estimates effect for compliers only (LATE), not everyone
  • Weak instruments → huge variance and bias
  • Exclusion violations are silent and fatal
Use caseDoes enabling Instant Book raise host earnings? Randomize an in-app encouragement to enable it; use the nudge as an instrument for adoption.
Propensity Score Matching (PSM)DEEP

Problem solved: purely observational adopter vs non-adopter comparisons. Model each unit's probability of treatment from pre-treatment covariates (the propensity score), then match treated units to untreated units with similar scores, mimicking a randomized comparison on observables.

Assumptions
  • Ignorability: no unobserved confounders given the covariates — the big one
  • Overlap: comparable untreated units exist across the score range
  • Covariates measured pre-treatment
Diagnostics
  • Standardized mean differences after matching (<0.1)
  • Propensity distribution overlap plot
  • Sensitivity analysis (Rosenbaum bounds / E-values)
Pros
  • Intuitive, auditable balance tables stakeholders trust
  • Reduces model dependence vs raw regression
  • Weighting variants (IPW, doubly-robust) improve efficiency
Cons
  • Cannot fix confounders you didn't measure — selection on motivation usually survives
  • Discards unmatched units (changes the population)
  • Many researcher degrees of freedom
Use caseEstimate the effect of hosts adding professional photos on bookings, matching on listing quality, price tier, market, and pre-period demand. Report as "associational with adjustment," not gospel.
Interrupted Time Series (ITS)DEEP

Problem solved: the change launched to everyone at once — no comparison group exists. Model the pre-launch trend (level, slope, seasonality) and measure the post-launch deviation from the projected counterfactual (segmented regression / Bayesian structural time series, e.g. CausalImpact).

Assumptions
  • Pre-trend model is correct and would have continued
  • No co-occurring shocks at launch (marketing pushes, press, outages)
  • Launch date known and sharp
Pros
  • Works when nothing else does (full launches, brand events)
  • Uses only the series' own history; covariate series improve it
Cons
  • Anything else happening at launch is absorbed into the "effect"
  • Needs long, stable history; weak for slow-burn effects
  • Autocorrelation complicates inference
Use caseAn app-wide checkout redesign ships to 100% on day X. Fit daily conversion for the prior 12 months, project forward, and read the post-launch gap with uncertainty bands.
Geo Experiments & Cluster RandomizationDEEP

Problem solved: user-level randomization is impossible (broadcast media, brand campaigns) or invalid (marketplace/network interference). Randomize self-contained clusters — cities, DMAs, regions, or graph communities — so interference stays inside units.

Assumptions
  • Limited spillover across cluster boundaries (commuting, travel, social ties)
  • Clusters comparable, or made so via stratification/matched pairs
  • Enough clusters for power (the binding constraint)
Pros
  • Captures equilibrium/market-level effects A/B misses
  • Only credible design for offline media incrementality
  • Pairs naturally with synthetic-control analysis (trimmed-match, GeoLift-style)
Cons
  • Tiny n (tens of geos) → low power; needs stratified/matched assignment + covariate adjustment
  • Expensive and operationally heavy
  • Heterogeneous geos make estimates noisy
Use caseExpedia measures TV-ad incrementality: match 30 DMA pairs on size and booking trends, treat one of each pair, compare booking lift per dollar.
Switchback ExperimentsDEEP

Problem solved: marketplace-wide levers (surge pricing, dispatch, ranking the whole market sees) where every user shares one state. Alternate the entire market between treatment and control across time windows (e.g., region × 30-min blocks), randomizing the window assignment.

Assumptions
  • Carryover between windows is short relative to window length (driver positioning, queued orders decay fast)
  • Effects stabilize within a window
  • Window assignment is randomized (often with balancing constraints)
Pros
  • Each market serves as its own control → time-invariant market differences cancel
  • Handles shared-state interference that user splits cannot
Cons
  • Carryover bias if windows are too short; power loss if too long
  • Observations within region-windows are correlated → cluster-robust inference required
  • Users may experience inconsistent behavior across windows
Use caseDoorDash compares two dispatch algorithms by alternating them per region per 30-minute block for two weeks, analyzing delivery time and Dasher utilization at the window level with CUPAC adjustment.
Staggered RolloutsDEEP

Problem solved: the decision to ship is made, but you still want risk control and a usable effect estimate. Roll out in waves (5% → 25% → 100% of users, or market by market); if wave order is randomized, later waves serve as temporary controls for earlier ones.

Assumptions
  • Wave order random (or as-good-as-random) — convenience ordering by market size confounds everything
  • No anticipation effects in not-yet-treated waves
  • For staggered DiD analysis: use heterogeneity-robust estimators
Pros
  • Risk containment built in: regressions caught at 5%
  • Measurement nearly free if order is randomized
  • Organizationally easy to sell
Cons
  • Confounded by time if order isn't random
  • Control pool shrinks each wave → power decays
  • Classic two-way FE on staggered adoption can even flip signs
Use caseA payments migration rolls out to randomized 10% user waves weekly; each wave's first week is compared against not-yet-migrated users for a clean read before 100%.
Holdout GroupsDEEP

Problem solved: individual A/B tests measure short-run, isolated effects. A long-term holdout keeps a small slice of users (0.5–5%) on the old experience for months while everyone else gets the full stream of launches — measuring cumulative, durable impact.

Design notes
  • Re-randomize periodically; a permanent holdout drifts out of comparability
  • Scope clearly: feature-level, team-level, or global holdout
  • Track novelty decay: cumulative effect vs sum of individual test wins
Pros
  • Catches interaction effects and novelty decay across many launches
  • The honest answer to "did this quarter's work move anything?"
Cons
  • Holdout users get a degraded product for months — ethics/CX cost
  • Engineering cost of keeping old paths alive
  • Contamination via cross-device, support scripts, word of mouth
Use caseMeta-style notification holdout: 1% of users receive the old notification stack for two quarters; the gap vs everyone else prices the whole roadmap, including annoyance-driven churn the individual tests missed.
Shadow TestingDEEP

Problem solved: a new model or system is too risky to expose. Run it in parallel on live traffic, log what it would have done, and compare offline against the production system — zero user exposure.

Pros
  • Zero user risk; validates latency, error rates, and prediction quality at production scale
  • Catches train/serve skew before any experiment
  • Cheap precursor that de-risks the real A/B
Cons
  • No behavioral outcomes — you see what would be shown, never how users respond
  • Feedback loops absent (a ranker changes what users click, which changes the data)
  • Tempting to over-claim from offline metrics
Use caseAirbnb shadows a new search-ranking model for two weeks: identical queries scored by both systems, comparing overlap, latency, and offline NDCG — then graduates it to a real experiment.
Natural ExperimentsDEEP

Problem solved: no design is available, but the world randomizes for you — outages, policy changes, weather, lotteries, arbitrary eligibility rules create as-if-random variation in treatment.

Assumptions
  • The shock is plausibly unrelated to potential outcomes (argue, don't assume)
  • Affected vs unaffected units otherwise comparable
  • Usually analyzed via DiD, IV, or RDD machinery
Pros
  • Real-world variation at scales you could never engineer
  • Sometimes the only ethical route (pricing harm, legal constraints)
Cons
  • Rare and opportunistic — you can't schedule one
  • As-if randomness is a claim, not a fact
  • Shock may bundle multiple changes at once
Use caseA 6-hour push-notification outage hits a random subset of devices: compare their next-week engagement vs unaffected devices to estimate notifications' true incremental value.
S8

Causal Inference

Deep dive

The reasoning layer beneath every method in S7. If you can read a DAG, you can predict which analyses are biased before running a single query.

Correlation vs CausationDEEP

X and Y can correlate because X→Y, Y→X (reverse causation), a third variable drives both (confounding), or you conditioned on a common effect (collider/selection). Only one of those four supports "ship X to move Y" — the entire job is telling them apart.

Example"Users with the app installed spend 3× more on the website." Install caused spend? High-intent users install apps? Or did a campaign target big spenders with install ads? Each story implies a different action.
Key takeawayA correlation is one observable consequence of many possible causal structures. Decide the structure first (draw the DAG), then pick the analysis.
ConfoundingDEEP

A confounder Z causes both treatment X and outcome Y, creating a non-causal "backdoor path" X ← Z → Y. The raw X–Y association mixes the true effect with Z's influence. Fix: block the backdoor by adjusting for Z (regression, matching, stratification) — or destroy it entirely with randomization, which deletes the Z→X arrow.

Fork · chain · collider — the three primitive structures
ExampleTrip intent (Z) drives both wishlist use (X) and booking (Y). Adjusting for intent proxies shrinks the wishlist "effect" from +28pp to +4pp.
Key takeawayAdjust for confounders (forks). Do not adjust for mediators (chains) if you want the total effect, and never adjust for colliders.
Selection BiasDEEP

The analyzed sample is conditioned on something caused by treatment, outcome, or both — surveys answered only by happy users, funnels analyzed only for completers, churned users excluded. Formally it's conditioning on a collider: sample inclusion is the common effect.

Example"NPS rose after the redesign" — but the redesign frustrated low-engagement users into leaving before the survey fired. The survivors were always happier.
Key takeawayAsk of every dataset: who is missing, and could the treatment or outcome have decided that? If yes, the comparison is biased before any statistics happen.
Collider BiasDEEP

X → C ← Y: X and Y both cause C. Unconditionally, X and Y can be independent — but condition on C (filter, stratify, or "control for" it) and a spurious X–Y association appears. The classic backwards intuition: adding more control variables can create bias.

ExampleAmong featured listings (featured if great photos OR low price), photo quality and price look negatively correlated — pure artifact of the filter. Across all listings, no such trade-off exists.
Key takeaway"Control for everything" is wrong. Each covariate is either a confounder (adjust), mediator (don't, for total effects), or collider (never). The DAG tells you which.
Simpson's ParadoxDEEP · INTERACTIVE

A trend that appears in aggregate reverses within every subgroup (or vice versa), because group membership confounds the relationship. Neither view is automatically "right" — the causal structure decides which one answers your question.

Toggle the view · same data, opposite conclusions

ExampleAggregate: more search filters used → lower booking rate. Within mobile and within desktop: more filters → higher booking rate. Mobile users use more filters and convert less for unrelated reasons — platform is the confounder.
Key takeawayIf group membership causally precedes both variables, the segmented view is the causal one. Decide by structure, not by which chart looks better.
DAGs (Directed Acyclic Graphs)DEEP

Nodes are variables; arrows are direct causal claims. DAGs make assumptions explicit and mechanical: list every path from treatment to outcome, classify each as causal (front-door) or spurious (backdoor), then choose an adjustment set that blocks all backdoor paths while leaving causal paths open — the backdoor criterion.

StructurePatternAdjusting for the middle node…
Fork (confounder)X ← Z → Y✅ Removes spurious association — adjust
Chain (mediator)X → M → Y⚠️ Blocks part of the real effect — don't, for total effect
ColliderX → C ← Y❌ Creates spurious association — never
Key takeawayFive minutes drawing the DAG with a domain expert beats five weeks of regression archaeology. Every S7 method is a strategy for blocking backdoor paths when you can't randomize them away.
S9

Advanced Product Analytics

Quick ref
Funnel · biggest drop ≠ biggest opportunity
Funnel AnalysisREF

Stepwise conversion through a flow. Read it with headroom in mind: a 95%→90% step has little to gain; a 40% step with strong intent upstream is where experiments pay.

Key takeawayPrioritize steps by reachable headroom × traffic, not by raw drop-off. And fix the funnel definition (time window, ordering, dedup) before comparing anything.
Cohort AnalysisREF

Group users by start period (or acquisition channel) and track each group over relative time. Separates real product change from mix shift — a "declining" average metric is often just newer, lower-quality cohorts diluting the blend.

CohortM0M1M2M3M4
Jan100%42%33%29%27%
Feb100%44%35%31%
Mar100%48%39%
Apr100%52%
Key takeawayRead down the columns: each cohort retaining better at the same age (42→44→48→52% at M1) is the cleanest observational evidence the product is improving.
RetentionREF

Define it three ways and say which: N-day (active exactly on day N), rolling/unbounded (active on day N or later), bracket (active within a window). The shape matters more than the level — a curve that flattens means a durable habit; one that decays to zero means a leaky bucket no acquisition can fill.

Key takeawayMatch the retention window to natural usage frequency: daily for messaging, weekly for food delivery, yearly for travel. Wrong window → false alarm or false comfort.
SegmentationREF

Behavioral segments (frequency, recency, use case) beat demographic ones for product decisions. Segments are most valuable defined before analysis; segments discovered after seeing results are multiple-testing bait (S3).

Key takeawayA good segmentation changes a decision (build, target, price). If every segment gets the same action, it's a chart, not a segmentation.
LTV (Lifetime Value)REF

Discounted margin a user generates over their lifetime. Estimate via cohort extrapolation (fit the retention curve, multiply by margin per active period) or survival/probabilistic models. The LTV/CAC ratio governs how aggressively you can acquire.

Key takeawayLTV is a model, not a metric — always quote the horizon and assumptions. "3-year contribution-margin LTV" is honest; "LTV" is negotiable fiction.
ChurnREF

For subscriptions churn is an event (cancel); for usage products it's a definition (inactive ≥ X days, with X chosen from natural return-gap distributions). Leading indicators — usage decay, support contacts, payment failures — beat the lagging churn flag for intervention.

Key takeawayPredicting churn is easy; preventing it is causal. The save-offer's value is its incremental effect on would-be churners — an uplift question, not a classification one.
AttributionREF

Assigning conversion credit across touchpoints. Rule-based (last-touch, first-touch, position) is arbitrary; data-driven (Shapley, Markov) is more principled but still correlational — it allocates observed credit, it doesn't prove causation.

Key takeawayLast-touch systematically over-credits bottom-funnel channels (brand search, retargeting). Use attribution for steering between channels, incrementality (below) for budget truth.
IncrementalityREF

The causal question behind attribution: how many conversions would not have happened without the spend? Measured with geo experiments, intent-to-treat ad holdouts, or ghost ads — never with click-through data alone.

ExampleRetargeting shows 8× ROAS by last-touch; a geo holdout shows 1.2× incremental ROAS. The difference is users who would have purchased anyway.
Key takeawayAttribution divides the pie; incrementality measures whether the pie got bigger.
Uplift ModelingREF

Predict the individual treatment effect (CATE) to target only "persuadables" — skip sure-things, lost causes, and the do-not-disturbs a treatment would backfire on. Trained on randomized experiment data (T-learner, X-learner, causal forests); evaluated with Qini/uplift curves, not AUC.

ExampleA discount lifts conversion +1% overall, but +9% on the top-uplift-decile users. Targeting that decile delivers most of the value at a tenth of the discount cost.
Key takeawayPropensity models target who will convert; uplift models target who will convert because of you. Different question, often opposite targets.
S10

Airbnb Case Studies

Illustrative

Six composite cases in the Airbnb domain, written to exercise the methods in S5–S7. Numbers are illustrative.

1 · Search Ranking ChangesCASE

ProblemRank listings to maximize bookings without starving new hosts of exposure or harming long-term marketplace health.

HypothesisAdding personalized booking-probability features to the ranker lifts search→booking conversion ≥1% with neutral guardrails.

MetricsPrimary: search→booking conversion. Guardrails: cancellation rate, new-host exposure share, result diversity, search latency. Diagnostics: CTR by position, booked-listing rank.

DesignNaive user-level A/B is contaminated: treatment users book scarce inventory away from control (S6 interference), inflating the lift. Plan: shadow-test the model first (S7), then user-level A/B read alongside market-level liquidity monitoring; in supply-constrained markets, validate with a market-level (geo) read.

DecisionConversion +1.4%, guardrails neutral, but new-host exposure −6%. Ship with an exploration term that floors new-listing impressions; re-test. Document the interference-adjusted estimate (+0.8%) as the expected launch impact.

2 · Dynamic Pricing (Smart Pricing)CASE

ProblemHosts misprice: empty calendars in low season, underpriced inventory at peak. Does algorithmic price guidance raise booked nights without cutting host revenue?

HypothesisHosts adopting Smart Pricing gain ≥3% booked nights at neutral-or-better revenue per listing.

MetricsPrimary: booked nights per listing. Guardrails: host revenue/night, host opt-out rate, guest price-complaint rate. Health: market price dispersion.

DesignListing-level randomization leaks: repricing some listings shifts demand to or from others in the same market (S6 spillover). Plan: cluster-randomize by market (S7 geo), stratified by market size and seasonality (S5); within-market price equilibrium is then captured, not contaminated. CUPED on pre-period booked nights for power with few clusters.

DecisionTreated markets: +2.6% booked nights, revenue/night −0.8% (within tolerance), opt-outs concentrated among premium hosts. Ship with a price-floor control for premium segments; monitor with a 5% market holdout.

3 · Host IncentivesCASE

ProblemSupply-constrained markets need new listings. Does a $100 listing bonus create incremental active hosts, or pay people who would have listed anyway?

HypothesisThe bonus raises new-listing activation ≥15% among prospects, at a cost-per-incremental-listing below 90-day listing LTV.

MetricsPrimary: activated listings (first booking-ready listing). Secondary: 90-day listing survival, incremental booked nights. Economics: cost per incremental activation.

DesignCan't compare bonus-claimers vs others — claiming is self-selected. Randomized encouragement (S7 IV): offer the bonus to a random subset of registered prospects; the offer is the instrument for completing activation. LATE gives the effect for prospects the bonus actually moved — exactly the population the spend targets.

DecisionOffer lifts activation 4.2pp on an 18% base; cost per incremental activation ≈ $620 vs $540 LTV. Don't scale flat. Retest a tiered bonus targeted by an uplift model (S9) trained on this experiment.

4 · Search FiltersCASE

ProblemRemote-work travelers can't find suitable stays. Does a "work-friendly" filter (verified wifi speed, desk) improve their conversion?

HypothesisAmong sessions that open the filter panel, exposing the new filter lifts booking conversion ≥2%.

MetricsPrimary: triggered-session booking conversion. Guardrails: overall conversion, zero-result search rate. Diagnostics: filter adoption, result-set size after filtering.

DesignExpected trigger rate ~6% of sessions — a raw all-users read would dilute a +3% triggered effect to +0.18% overall and need months of traffic. Triggered analysis (S5) with counterfactual logging: control sessions are flagged the moment the filter panel opens. SRM checked within the triggered population.

DecisionTriggered conversion +2.7% (significant); diluted launch impact ≈ +0.16% overall bookings, zero-result rate +0.4pp in low-supply markets. Ship, but suppress the filter where verified-wifi supply < threshold; start a host wifi-verification push.

5 · Instant BookingCASE

ProblemLeadership wants "the effect of Instant Book on host earnings." Adoption is a host choice — responsive, professional hosts adopt more, confounding every raw comparison.

HypothesisEnabling Instant Book raises a host's booked nights ≥5% via lower booking friction and a ranking boost.

MetricsPrimary: booked nights per listing. Secondary: host cancellation rate, guest rebooking. Guardrail: host churn.

DesignThree layers of evidence. (1) PSM (S7) on quality, tenure, market, and pre-period demand for a fast adjusted estimate — labeled as observational. (2) Randomized encouragement (IV) nudging a random host subset to enable, for a causal LATE. (3) For the long-run marketplace question, a market-level holdout where IB ranking boosts are withheld.

DecisionPSM said +11%; IV said +6% — the gap is selection bias, quantified. Report +6% as causal for movable hosts; use it for adoption-campaign ROI, and keep the holdout running for the ecosystem read.

6 · Recommendation Algorithm ChangesCASE

ProblemA new "similar listings" carousel model. Does it create bookings, or cannibalize search?

HypothesisThe new model lifts net bookings per visitor ≥0.5%, not just carousel engagement.

MetricsPrimary: bookings per visitor (net, surface-agnostic). Guardrails: search conversion (cannibalization check), latency. Diagnostics: carousel CTR, share of bookings via carousel.

DesignStage 1: shadow test (S7) for latency, coverage, and offline ranking quality. Stage 2: user-level A/B with the net metric primary — carousel CTR alone would reward stealing clicks from search. Monitor effect-by-exposure-day to separate novelty (S3) from durable lift; plan a 4-week minimum read.

DecisionCarousel CTR +18%, but net bookings +0.1% (ns) and search conversion −0.6%: mostly cannibalization with a novelty spike decaying after week 2. Iterate on candidate diversity rather than ship; keep the net-bookings primary for the rerun.
