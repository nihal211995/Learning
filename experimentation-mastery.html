Section 02

Experimentation Fundamentals

Why randomized experiments are the gold standard for causal claims, and the vocabulary every experimenter must own before touching a calculator.

causalitycounterfactualrandomizationOEC

The counterfactual problem

The causal effect of a feature on a user is the difference between two worlds: the world where they got the feature and the world where they didn't. We can never observe both for the same user at the same time — this is the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference. Randomized experiments solve it at the group level: random assignment makes treatment and control statistically identical in expectation, so the control group is a valid stand-in for the treatment group's counterfactual.

Airbnb exampleAirbnb wants to know whether showing "Rare find — this place is usually booked" increases bookings. Comparing users who saw it vs. users who didn't (observationally) is biased: the badge only appears on high-demand listings, which already convert better. Randomizing whether eligible users see the badge isolates the badge's causal effect.

Why correlation isn't enough

  • Confounding: a third variable drives both treatment and outcome (demand drives both badge exposure and bookings).
  • Selection bias: users self-select into exposure (power users adopt new features first and already retain better).
  • Reverse causality: the outcome causes the "treatment" (people who book more open the app more, not vice versa).

Anatomy of an experiment

What experiments can and cannot do

Strengths

  • Gold-standard internal validity
  • Quantifies effect size + uncertainty
  • Detects unintended harm via guardrails
  • Builds institutional learning velocity

Limits

  • Measures short-run effects unless long-running holdouts exist
  • Breaks under interference / network effects
  • Can't test irreversible, ethical, or whole-market changes
  • Local optimization risk: hill-climbing to a local maximum
Interview question"Your PM says: users who use the new wishlist feature retain 40% better — let's force it on everyone." How do you respond? Answer in spirit: self-selection — engaged users adopt features; the comparison is confounded by engagement. Propose a randomized rollout (or staggered rollout/holdout) to measure incremental retention.
Section 03

Statistical Foundations

Eighteen concepts, each with definition, intuition, formula, business interpretation, an Airbnb example, the common mistake, and an interview probe. This is the bedrock of every experimentation interview.

ProbabilityP(A)

Definition. A measure in [0,1] of how likely an event is; the long-run relative frequency (frequentist) or degree of belief (Bayesian).

Visual intuition. Picture 10,000 searchers; if 900 book, the empirical booking probability is 0.09. As n grows, the relative frequency stabilizes around the true probability.

P(A) = favorable outcomes / total outcomes (uniform) P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Business interpretation. Conversion rates, churn rates, CTRs are all estimated probabilities — treat them as estimates with uncertainty, not constants.

AirbnbP(booking | search) ≈ 0.09 means roughly 9 in 100 searchers complete a booking; the OEC of many search experiments.
Common mistakeAdding probabilities of non-mutually-exclusive events (e.g., P(uses app) + P(uses web) > 1 because many users do both).
Interview"If 60% of guests use mobile and 50% use web, what's the minimum % using both?" → 10% (inclusion–exclusion, capped at 100%).
Conditional ProbabilityP(A|B)

Definition. Probability of A given that B occurred — restrict the sample space to B.

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Visual intuition. Zoom into the slice of users who did B; ask what fraction also did A.

Business interpretation. Funnel rates are conditional probabilities: P(book | viewed listing) ≠ P(book | searched).

AirbnbP(book | added to wishlist) = 0.22 vs P(book) = 0.09 — wishlisting is a high-intent signal, but it does not mean wishlists cause bookings.
Common mistakeConfusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) — the "prosecutor's fallacy". P(fraud | flagged) ≠ P(flagged | fraud).
Interview"Your fraud model has 99% recall and flags 5% of bookings; 0.5% of bookings are fraudulent. What fraction of flags are real fraud?" → ≈ 0.99×0.005/0.05 ≈ 10%.
Bayes' Theoremposterior

Definition. Mechanism to invert conditional probabilities — update a prior belief with evidence.

P(H|E) = P(E|H) · P(H) / P(E) P(E) = P(E|H)P(H) + P(E|¬H)P(¬H)

Visual intuition. Natural frequencies: out of 10,000 bookings, 50 fraudulent; model catches 49 of them but also flags ~498 legit → among 547 flags, only ~9% are fraud.

Business interpretation. Base rates dominate when events are rare — fraud detection, anomaly alerts, even "is this metric movement real?"

AirbnbAn alert fires when daily bookings drop 3σ. Even with a good detector, most alerts in stable systems are false alarms because true incidents are rare — investigate with priors in mind.
Common mistakeIgnoring the base rate P(H). A "99% accurate" test on a 1-in-1000 condition yields mostly false positives.
Interview"A test variant shows p = 0.04. What's the probability the effect is real?" → Cannot answer from p alone; depends on the prior probability of true effects (Bayesian framing of false discovery).
Random Variables & DistributionsX ~ F

Definition. A random variable maps outcomes to numbers; its distribution describes which values occur with what probability. Key families: Bernoulli (convert / not), Binomial (count of conversions in n trials), Poisson (event counts per period), Normal (sums and averages), Log-normal (revenue, nights, heavy right tails).

Bernoulli(p): E[X]=p, Var=p(1−p) Binomial(n,p): E=np, Var=np(1−p) Poisson(λ): E=Var=λ

Business interpretation. Choosing the right test starts with recognizing the metric's distribution: proportions → z-test on rates; heavy-tailed revenue → t-test on means is fragile, consider trimming/winsorizing, bootstrap, or log transforms (carefully).

AirbnbBooking value per user is zero-inflated and log-normal-ish: most users book nothing; a few book luxury villas. The mean is dragged by whales — a single $20k booking can flip a naïve test.
Common mistakeAssuming normality of the data is required for a t-test. What matters is approximate normality of the sample mean (CLT) — but heavy tails slow CLT convergence dramatically.
Interview"Your revenue metric has extreme outliers. Options?" → winsorize/cap, analyze quantiles, log(1+x) with care about interpretation, bootstrap CIs, or use a binarized companion metric.
Expected ValueE[X]

Definition. Probability-weighted average of a random variable — the long-run mean.

E[X] = Σ xᵢ·P(xᵢ) (discrete) E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y] (always, even if dependent)

Business interpretation. Expected LTV, expected revenue per visitor, expected cost of an incident — the currency of decision-making under uncertainty.

AirbnbExpected revenue per searcher = P(book)×avg booking value×take rate = 0.09 × $400 × 0.15 ≈ $5.40 — the number a ranking experiment is ultimately trying to move.
Common mistakeForgetting linearity holds regardless of independence — but E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] requires independence (or zero covariance).
Interview"A promo costs $10 per redeemer and lifts booking probability from 9% to 11% on a $60 contribution margin. Worth it?" → ΔE = 0.02×60 − 10×P(redeem)… set up the expected-value algebra, ask for redemption rate.
Variance & Standard Deviationσ²

Definition. Expected squared deviation from the mean; SD is its square root, in original units.

Var(X) = E[(X−μ)²] = E[X²] − (E[X])² Var(aX+b) = a²Var(X) Var(X+Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y)+2Cov(X,Y) Var(X̄) = σ²/n

Business interpretation. Variance is the enemy of experiment sensitivity: required sample size scales linearly with metric variance. Everything in variance reduction (CUPED, stratification) exists because of σ².

Airbnb"Nights booked per user" has far higher variance than "booked? yes/no". Tests on the binary metric reach significance much faster — one reason teams pair a conversion metric with a value metric.
Common mistakeDividing by n instead of n−1 for sample variance is minor; the big one is ignoring covariance when combining metrics (Var of a ratio ≠ ratio of variances → delta method).
Interview"Why does CUPED reduce required sample size?" → It removes the share of variance explained by pre-experiment behavior: σ²(1−ρ²).
Covariance & Correlationρ

Definition. Covariance measures co-movement (unit-bound); correlation is covariance standardized to [−1, 1].

Cov(X,Y) = E[(X−μx)(Y−μy)] ρ = Cov(X,Y) / (σx·σy)

Business interpretation. Correlation between pre-period and in-experiment metrics powers CUPED; correlation between metrics determines how much "independent information" a dashboard really has.

AirbnbA user's bookings last quarter correlate ρ≈0.6 with bookings this quarter — that pre-period signal is exactly what CUPED exploits for ~36% variance reduction.
Common mistakeReading ρ=0 as independence (only true for joint normal); and forgetting correlation is linear-only — a U-shape can have ρ≈0.
Interview"Two metrics both moved +2% and are correlated 0.9 — is that two pieces of evidence?" → Largely one; effective evidence depends on their correlation.
Central Limit TheoremCLT

Definition. Sums/means of many independent draws are approximately Normal, regardless of the underlying distribution, with variance σ²/n.

X̄ ≈ Normal(μ, σ²/n) as n → ∞ Z = (X̄ − μ) / (σ/√n)

Visual intuition. Even a Bernoulli coin (two spikes) yields a bell curve of sample means once n is a few hundred.

Business interpretation. CLT is why z/t tests work on conversion rates and revenue means at internet scale — and why a single percentage difference comes with a Normal confidence band.

AirbnbPer-user revenue is wildly skewed, but the average over 500k users per arm is near-Normal — though heavy tails mean convergence is slower and outlier handling still matters.
Common mistakeInvoking CLT with tiny n on skewed data, or with correlated observations (sessions within users) where the effective n is much smaller.
Interview"Why can you use a z-test on a 2% conversion rate?" → The sampling distribution of p̂ is approximately Normal by CLT when np and n(1−p) are large.
Confidence Intervals95% CI

Definition. A procedure that, across repeated samples, captures the true parameter X% of the time. A 95% CI for a lift is the range of effects consistent with the data.

CI = estimate ± z₍₁₋α/₂₎ · SE Diff in proportions: SE = √(p₁(1−p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1−p₂)/n₂)

Business interpretation. Report lifts as intervals, not points: "+1.4% [+0.2%, +2.6%]" tells a launch decision far more than "p < 0.05".

AirbnbA pricing-tip experiment shows host adoption +3.0% with CI [+0.4%, +5.6%]: significant, but the lower bound matters if the business case needs ≥2%.
Common mistake"There's a 95% probability the true effect is in this interval" — that's the Bayesian credible-interval interpretation; the frequentist statement is about the procedure across repetitions.
Interview"CI includes zero but the point estimate looks great — ship?" → Discuss power, practical significance vs statistical, cost of waiting for more data, and pre-registered decision rules.
Hypothesis TestingH₀ vs H₁

Definition. Frame a null (no effect) and alternative; compute how surprising the data is under the null; reject if surprise exceeds threshold α.

H₀: p_T = p_C H₁: p_T ≠ p_C z = (p̂_T − p̂_C) / SE_pooled ; reject if |z| > 1.96 (α=0.05)

Business interpretation. The α threshold is a business decision about false-launch tolerance, not a law of nature. Two-sided is default; one-sided needs strong justification and pre-registration.

AirbnbFor irreversible, high-cost launches (changing the cancellation policy), teams may demand α=0.01; for cheap UI tweaks, α=0.10 with a fast iteration loop can be rational.
Common mistakeTreating "fail to reject" as "no effect". Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence — check power and the CI width before declaring neutrality.
Interview"What null would you use for a non-inferiority launch (cheaper infra, hope no harm)?" → H₀: treatment is worse by more than margin δ; flip the burden of proof.
Type I Error (False Positive)α

Definition. Rejecting H₀ when it is true — declaring a win that doesn't exist. Controlled at rate α per test.

α = P(reject H₀ | H₀ true), conventionally 0.05

Business interpretation. Cost = shipping a useless/harmful feature, polluting the roadmap with false learnings, eroding trust in experimentation.

AirbnbRunning 100 ranking experiments a quarter at α=0.05 with mostly-null ideas → ~5 false "wins" ship per quarter unless multiple-testing discipline exists.
Common mistakePeeking daily and stopping at the first p<0.05 inflates the real α to 25–30%+. Fix with sequential methods (Section 9).
Interview"Team ran one test, got p=0.049, celebrated. Concerns?" → Peeking? Multiple metrics? Multiple segments? One-off p just under threshold is weak evidence; consider replication.
Type II Error (False Negative) & Powerβ, 1−β

Definition. Failing to detect a real effect (Type II, rate β). Power = 1−β = probability of detecting an effect of a given size if it exists. Convention: 80%.

Power = P(reject H₀ | true effect = δ) Power ↑ with: n ↑, δ ↑, σ ↓, α ↑

Business interpretation. Underpowered tests kill good ideas silently and produce exaggerated estimates when they do "win" (winner's curse / Type M error).

AirbnbA host-side experiment on superhost nudges only has 40k eligible hosts; at that n, MDE may be 5% when the plausible effect is 1% — the test is doomed before launch. Better: redesign metric, extend duration, or use variance reduction.
Common mistakeComputing "post-hoc power" from the observed effect — it's a 1:1 transform of the p-value and adds zero information.
Interview"Test was neutral. PM asks: so the feature does nothing?" → "We can rule out effects larger than X (the MDE); smaller effects remain possible."
P-valuesp

Definition. Probability of observing data at least this extreme, assuming H₀ is true. It is NOT the probability H₀ is true, NOT the probability of replication, NOT effect size.

p = P(|Z| ≥ |z_obs| | H₀)

Business interpretation. A tiny p with a tiny effect at huge n can be practically meaningless; a moderate p on a large effect at small n can be a strong bet. Always pair p with the CI and the pre-specified practical threshold.

AirbnbWith 10M users/arm, a +0.05% conversion lift can hit p<0.001 — statistically real, but possibly below the cost of maintaining the feature. Significance ≠ ship.
Common mistakep=0.06 → "no effect"; p=0.04 → "effect". The 0.05 cliff is a convention; the difference between p=0.04 and p=0.06 is itself not significant.
Interview"Explain a p-value to a PM in one sentence." → "If the feature truly did nothing, we'd see a difference this big or bigger only p% of the time — small p means the data is hard to explain by luck alone."
Multiple Testingm tests

Definition. Running many hypothesis tests (metrics × segments × variants × peeks) inflates the chance of at least one false positive.

P(≥1 false positive) = 1 − (1−α)^m m=20, α=0.05 → 64%

Business interpretation. Segment fishing ("it works for iOS users in Brazil!") is the most common way experimentation programs deceive themselves.

AirbnbA neutral experiment is sliced by 8 markets × 3 platforms × 2 user types = 48 looks; ~2–3 "significant" cells appear by chance. Pre-register primary metric + a small set of segments; treat the rest as hypothesis-generating.
Common mistakeApplying corrections to guardrails the same way as success metrics — for guardrails you often want MORE sensitivity to harm, not less.
Interview"Your test shows a win only in one of 12 segments. What now?" → Treat as exploratory; replicate with a pre-registered targeted test before shipping segment-specific.
Bonferroni Correctionα/m

Definition. Control family-wise error rate (FWER) by testing each of m hypotheses at α/m.

α* = α / m (e.g., 0.05/10 = 0.005 per test)

Business interpretation. Bulletproof but blunt: power collapses as m grows. Holm's step-down is uniformly better; use FWER when ANY false positive is costly (e.g., declaring a winning variant among many).

AirbnbTesting 5 ranking variants against control: Bonferroni at 0.01 each protects against crowning a false winner of the bake-off.
Common mistakeUsing Bonferroni across 50 dashboard metrics — power evaporates; FDR control (next) fits exploratory settings better.
Interview"When Bonferroni vs BH?" → FWER (Bonferroni/Holm) for confirmatory decisions; FDR (BH) for exploratory screens where some false positives are tolerable.
False Discovery Rate & Benjamini–HochbergFDR

Definition. FDR = expected fraction of declared discoveries that are false. BH procedure: sort p-values ascending, find the largest k with p₍k₎ ≤ (k/m)·q, reject all hypotheses up to k.

BH: reject H₍₁₎…H₍k₎ where k = max{i : p₍i₎ ≤ (i/m)·q}

Business interpretation. "Of the experiments we call winners this quarter, at most ~10% are flukes" — the right guarantee for a high-velocity experimentation platform.

AirbnbScanning 200 candidate ranking features for signal: BH at q=0.10 surfaces a shortlist where ~90% of surfaced features are real, to be confirmed in dedicated tests.
Common mistakeReporting BH-adjusted results as if each individual finding carries 95% confidence — FDR is a property of the set, not each member.
Interview"You run 100 tests; 10 hit p<0.05. How many are real?" → Unknown without prior true-effect rate; if all nulls were true you'd expect 5 false hits — the observed 10 suggests some signal, motivating FDR thinking.
Statistical Power in Practicedesign

Definition. Power analysis done BEFORE launch fixes the experiment's resolution: smallest effect reliably detectable (MDE) given traffic and duration.

n per arm ≈ 2σ²(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² / δ² ≈ 16σ²/δ² (α=.05, power=.80 shortcut)

Business interpretation. The 16σ²/δ² shortcut makes the trade explicit: halving the MDE quadruples sample size. Power planning is roadmap planning.

AirbnbBaseline conversion 9%, want to detect +0.3pp absolute: n ≈ 16×0.09×0.91/0.003² ≈ 145k per arm — feasible in days on guest-side, weeks-to-impossible on host-side.
Common mistakeSetting MDE to "whatever significance we can get" instead of the smallest effect that would change the launch decision.
Interview"Traffic supports MDE of 3% but plausible effect is 1%. Options?" → Variance reduction (CUPED, stratification), longer duration, more sensitive proxy metric, triggered analysis, sequential design, or accept and reframe the decision.
Simpson's Paradox (preview)paradox

Definition. A trend present in subgroups reverses when groups are pooled, due to a confounder correlated with group sizes. Full treatment + simulation in Section 13 & 17.

AirbnbTreatment wins in both mobile and desktop, but loses overall — because treatment users skewed toward mobile, which has lower baseline conversion. Randomization imbalance or triggered-exposure differences are usual suspects.
Common mistakeResolving it mechanically ("always trust segments" or "always trust pooled") — the right level depends on the causal structure of the confounder.

A/B Testing Deep Dive

The complete operating manual: units, lifecycle, instrumentation checks, and the analysis decisions that separate trustworthy results from noise.

The experiment lifecycle

  1. Problem definition. What user/business problem? What does success look like? What decision will the experiment inform? (If no decision changes with the result, don't run it.)
  2. Hypothesis. "Because we observed [evidence], we believe [change] for [population] will cause [impact], measured by [metric] moving [direction, size]."
  3. Metric selection. One primary OEC, 2–4 secondary, explicit guardrails. Pre-register.
  4. Design. Unit, targeting/trigger condition, traffic split, power analysis → duration, exclusions, ramp plan.
  5. Launch & ramp. 1% → 5% → 50% with automated guardrail checks at each stage.
  6. Monitoring. Sample ratio, crash rates, latency, guardrails — NOT daily p-value peeking on the OEC.
  7. Analysis. Fixed-horizon (or pre-registered sequential) readout; segments as exploratory.
  8. Decision & learning. Ship / iterate / kill; write the result into a searchable knowledge base — neutral results are institutional gold.

Trust checks before reading the result

Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM)critical

If you configured 50/50 and got 50.6/49.4 on millions of users, the assignment or logging is broken. Chi-square test the observed split; p < 0.001 → halt analysis, debug.

χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected

Common causes: bot filtering applied unevenly, redirects dropping treatment users, triggered logging bugs, data pipeline joins dropping rows differently per arm. An experiment with SRM is not salvageable by clever stats — fix and rerun.

A/A testsplatform trust

Run control-vs-control: ~5% of A/A tests should be "significant" at α=0.05. If 20% are, your variance estimation is broken (often the unit-of-analysis mismatch). A/A also validates instrumentation, randomization independence across experiments, and CUPED implementations.

Novelty & primacy effectstime dynamics

Novelty: users click the shiny new thing; effect decays. Primacy: users need time to learn the change; effect grows. Diagnose by plotting the treatment effect by user-tenure-in-experiment (not by calendar day, which confounds with mix shift). Mitigations: run 2+ weekly cycles, analyze week-2+ cohorts, or use long-term holdouts.

Triggered analysis & dilutionsensitivity

If only 10% of assigned users ever reach the changed surface (e.g., the cancellation flow), analyzing all assigned users dilutes the effect 10× and destroys power. Triggered analysis restricts to users who hit the trigger condition — which must be defined identically in both arms (counterfactual triggering: log who would have seen the change in control). Then scale insights back: site-wide impact = triggered effect × trigger rate.

Analyzing results

Proportion metrics

z = (p̂_T−p̂_C)/√(p̂(1−p̂)(1/n_T+1/n_C))

Two-proportion z-test; report absolute and relative lift with CI.

Mean metrics

t = (X̄_T−X̄_C)/√(s²_T/n_T+s²_C/n_C)

Welch's t-test (unequal variances default). Outlier policy decided pre-launch.

Ratio metrics (e.g., CTR = clicks/impressions, randomized by user)

Var(Y̅/X̅) ≈ (μy²/μx²)·[Var(Y)/μy² + Var(X)/μx² − 2Cov(X,Y)/(μxμy)] / n

The delta method accounts for user-level correlation between numerator and denominator. Naïve per-impression analysis understates variance because impressions within a user are correlated.

Common pitfalls checklist

  • Peeking without sequential correction → inflated false positives
  • Stopping early on a win, extending on a loss → asymmetric bias
  • Segment fishing post-hoc → multiple testing
  • Ignoring SRM → garbage in, garbage out
  • Sub-weekly tests → day-of-week confounding (Airbnb demand is highly weekly-seasonal)
  • Interference between concurrent experiments → factorial assignment or layered platform design
  • Survivorship in metrics ("revenue per retained user") → condition-on-post-treatment bias
Section 05

Experiment Design Framework

A reusable 8-step template — the same structure interviewers expect when they say "design an experiment for X."

The interview-ready template

  1. Clarify the goal. What problem, for whom, and what decision does this inform? Ask 2–3 sharp clarifying questions.
  2. Hypothesis. Mechanism-based: change → behavior → metric. State expected direction and rough magnitude.
  3. Metrics. Primary OEC (1), secondaries (2–4), guardrails (cancellations, support contacts, latency, crashes, supply-side health). State the metric's unit and aggregation.
  4. Unit & population. Randomization unit, trigger condition, eligible population, exclusions (employees, bots, existing experimenters if interactions feared).
  5. Power. Baseline, MDE tied to the business case, α/power → n → duration (round to whole weeks).
  6. Risks. Interference/network effects? Novelty? Seasonality? Two-sided marketplace spillover? Ethical/UX risk? Mitigations for each.
  7. Launch plan. Ramp stages, monitoring plan, kill-switch criteria.
  8. Decision rule. Pre-commit: ship if primary ≥ X with no guardrail regression beyond Y; iterate if…; kill if…
Worked example — "Should Airbnb show total price (incl. fees) upfront in search?" Clarify: all markets or where regulation forces it anyway? Web+app? Hypothesis: price transparency reduces sticker shock at checkout → fewer abandons → higher booking conversion, possibly lower listing CTR. Metrics: primary = booking conversion per searcher; secondary = checkout abandonment, listing CTR, average booking value; guardrails = cancellation rate, support contacts, host-side new bookings distribution. Unit: guest user ID, triggered on search. Power: baseline 9%, MDE +0.25pp → ~2 weeks at 50/50 on eligible traffic. Risks: sellers may game cleaning fees (host-side behavior shift is slower than the test window → plan a long-term holdout); strong country heterogeneity → pre-register market segments. Decision: ship if conversion neutral-or-positive AND abandonment ↓, since regulatory tailwinds make neutrality acceptable.
Section 06

Metrics Design

Metrics are the contract between an experiment and a business decision. Good ones are sensitive, directional, hard to game, and connected to long-term value.

The three-tier system

North Star / OEC

The decision metric. Tied to durable value.

  • Booking conversion rate
  • Nights booked per searcher
  • Gross booking value
  • 4-week guest retention

Guardrails

Must-not-harm. Asymmetric: sensitive to regression.

  • Cancellation rate
  • Customer support contacts
  • App crashes / latency (p95)
  • Host complaints & churn
  • Refund rate

Diagnostics

Explain WHY the OEC moved. Never ship on these alone.

  • Search CTR
  • Search success rate (click in top 10)
  • Funnel step conversion
  • Pages per session

Properties of a good experiment metric (STEDII-style)

PropertyQuestionAnti-example
SensitiveDoes it move within experiment timescales?Annual retention in a 2-week test
TrustworthyIs logging robust and unbiased per arm?Client-side events lost when crashes differ by arm
DirectionalDoes up = good, unambiguously?Time-on-site (engagement or confusion?)
EfficientLow variance relative to signal?Raw revenue with whale outliers
DebuggableDecomposable into a metric tree?Opaque composite scores
UngameableDoes optimizing it create perverse incentives?CTR → clickbait ranking

Metric tree — Nights Booked

Nights booked = Visitors × P(search|visit) × P(view|search) × P(request|view) × P(accept|request) × nights/booking Each experiment claims one or two links of this chain; the OEC verifies the chain end-to-end.

Proxy metrics & surrogates

When the true goal (12-month LTV, long-term retention) is too slow, validated proxies bridge the gap. A good proxy is causally upstream and historically correlated with the goal under intervention, not just observationally. Netflix's classic example: hours viewed in week 1 as a surrogate for retention. Validate proxies with meta-analysis across past experiments: do treatments that moved the proxy also move the north star in long-running holdouts?

Goodhart's law"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Search CTR optimized directly yields seductive-photo rankings that book worse. Pair every optimizable proxy with downstream truth metrics.
Interview question"Airbnb's search team wants to use CTR as their OEC. Argue for/against." → Sensitive and fast (pro); gameable, weakly tied to bookings, ignores post-click quality (con). Better: booking conversion with CTR as diagnostic, or a learned relevance metric validated against bookings.
Section 07

Power Analysis

Power is the probability your experiment detects an effect that's really there. Plan it before launch or pay for it with false negatives and exaggerated winners.

The four-way trade-off

α (significance), power (1−β), effect size δ, and sample size n are locked together — fix any three and the fourth is determined. The working formula for a two-sample comparison of means:

n per arm = 2(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² σ² / δ² α=0.05, power=0.80 → (1.96+0.84)² = 7.85 → n ≈ 15.7σ²/δ² ≈ 16σ²/δ²

For proportions, σ² = p(1−p) at the baseline rate. The deepest intuition: n scales with 1/δ² — detecting an effect half as big costs 4× the sample.

Sequential Testing

Fixed-horizon tests forbid peeking; businesses peek anyway. Sequential methods make continuous monitoring statistically legal.

The peeking problem

Check a fixed-horizon test daily and stop at the first p < 0.05, and your real false-positive rate balloons — with daily peeks over a month, an honest α of 0.05 becomes roughly 25–30%. The p-value process is a random walk; given enough looks, it will dip below any threshold by chance. (Simulate it yourself in Section 17.)

Section 10

Variance Reduction Techniques

Same traffic, more sensitivity. These methods buy effective sample size with math instead of time — the highest-ROI skill in industrial experimentation.

CUPED — Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data

Why variance reduction matters: required n scales with σ². If pre-experiment behavior explains part of σ², removing it shrinks n proportionally — often the difference between a 4-week and a 2-week test.

The idea. A user's pre-experiment metric X (e.g., bookings last 4 weeks) predicts their in-experiment metric Y but is — by randomization — independent of assignment. Subtracting the predictable component leaves a less noisy metric with the same expected treatment effect.

Ŷᶜᵘᵖᵉᵈ = Y − θ(X − X̄), θ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X) Var(Ŷᶜᵘᵖᵉᵈ) = Var(Y)·(1 − ρ²ₓᵧ) ρ = 0.5 → 25% less variance ; ρ = 0.7 → 49% less ; ρ = 0.9 → 81% less

Worked example. Metric: nights booked over the 2-week test. Covariate: nights booked in the prior 8 weeks, ρ = 0.6. Variance reduction = 36% → required n falls 36%, or equivalently MDE shrinks by √(1−0.36) = 20% at fixed n.

Benefits

  • Unbiased: E[Ŷᶜᵘᵖᵉᵈ_T − Ŷᶜᵘᵖᵉᵈ_C] equals the true effect
  • Cheap: one covariance computation
  • Stacks with stratification and triggered analysis
  • θ estimated pooled across arms (standard, avoids bias)

Limitations

  • New users have no pre-period → impute X̄ or 0 (they get no reduction)
  • Weak ρ (new metrics, volatile behavior) → little gain
  • Wrong implementation (per-arm θ, post-treatment covariates) can bias results
  • Covariate must be pre-exposure — anything affected by treatment is forbidden

CUPAC — CUPED with ML predictions

Replace the single covariate with an ML model's prediction of Y from many pre-experiment features (DoorDash's formulation). The control variate becomes ŷ = f(pre-period features); reduction depends on the model's out-of-sample R². Same unbiasedness logic — the model must use only pre-exposure data and be trained out-of-experiment.

Stratified randomization

Pre-divide the population into homogeneous strata and randomize within each — guarantees balance on stratum variables instead of hoping for it. Variance falls by the between-strata share of total variance. Most valuable when n is small (host-side tests, B2B) where chance imbalance is a real risk.

Heterogeneous Treatment Effects (HTE)

The average effect can hide structure: +2% overall may be +8% for new users and −1% for power users. Estimation: pre-registered subgroup CIs (honest but limited), or causal ML — causal forests, T/X/S-learners, uplift models (Section 15) — with honest sample splitting. Discipline: HTE found post-hoc are hypotheses, not results; confirm in a follow-up targeted experiment.

Quantile Treatment Effects

The mean isn't the whole story for skewed metrics. QTE estimates the effect at the median, p90, p99 — "the feature didn't change average spend but compressed the left tail (fewer zero-booking users)." Computed via quantile regression or bootstrap of quantile differences. Caution: QTE compares marginal distributions, not the same user's quantile movement.

Causal Inference Methods

The conceptual machinery for causal claims without (or beyond) randomization: DAGs, confounding, and the biases that masquerade as findings.

Correlation vs. causation — the formal gap

In Rubin's potential-outcomes notation, each unit has Y(1) and Y(0); the causal effect is E[Y(1) − Y(0)]. Observational data gives E[Y|T=1] − E[Y|T=0], which equals the causal effect only if treatment is independent of potential outcomes — exactly what randomization buys and self-selection destroys.

Observed gap = ATT + selection bias E[Y|T=1] − E[Y|T=0] = E[Y(1)−Y(0)|T=1] + (E[Y(0)|T=1] − E[Y(0)|T=0])

DAGs — directed acyclic graphs

Nodes are variables; arrows are direct causal influence. Three primitive structures determine everything about when to adjust:

Chain (mediator)

T → M → Y

M transmits the effect. Controlling for M blocks the causal path — don't adjust if you want the total effect.

Fork (confounder)

T ← C → Y

C creates a spurious association. Must adjust for C (close the backdoor path).

Collider

T → K ← Y

K is jointly caused. Adjusting for K creates spurious association out of nothing — never condition on colliders.

Backdoor criterionTo identify T→Y, adjust for a set Z that blocks all backdoor paths (paths into T) and contains no descendants of T. This single rule replaces "control for everything" — which is wrong precisely because of colliders and mediators.

The bias bestiary

Confoundingfork

A common cause of treatment and outcome. Airbnb: hosts who enable Instant Book have better calendars and faster responses — comparing IB to non-IB hosts confounds the feature with host quality. Fix: randomize, or adjust for the confounders you can measure and argue about the ones you can't (sensitivity analysis).

Selection biassampling

The analyzed sample isn't representative of the target population because selection relates to treatment or outcome. Airbnb: surveying guests who completed trips about a new cancellation policy misses everyone the policy caused to cancel. Analyzing only users who opted into a beta confounds the feature with opt-in enthusiasm.

Survivorship biasselection on Y

Conditioning on having "survived" to be observed. Classic: WWII bombers — armor where returning planes were NOT hit. Airbnb: "hosts who use Smart Pricing earn 12% more" computed among active hosts excludes hosts whom bad pricing drove off the platform. Retention analyses on month-12 actives systematically flatter every feature.

Collider bias / Berkson's paradoxconditioning

Conditioning on a common effect induces spurious (often negative) correlation. Airbnb: among listings on the search first page (a collider — ranked high because of EITHER great reviews OR low price), price and review quality look negatively correlated even if independent overall. Any analysis "among bookings" or "among top results" risks this.

Simpson's paradoxaggregation

Subgroup trends reverse on pooling because a confounder is correlated with group size. Airbnb: a new checkout flow wins in every country, loses overall — because rollout skewed toward low-conversion emerging markets. Resolution requires the causal structure: if country affects assignment (or exposure mix), the stratified estimate is right; if treatment changes the mix itself, neither naive view is right. Simulate it in Section 17.

Interview question"Among booked trips, longer lead time correlates with lower review scores. Does booking early cause bad trips?" → "Among booked trips" conditions on a collider (booking); lead time also proxies trip type (big planned group trips vs spontaneous getaways) — confounding plus collider risk. Propose randomized nudges or within-user comparisons instead.

Experimentation When A/B Testing Is Not Possible

Fifteen methods for causal measurement without individual randomization — the section that separates senior candidates. Start with the decision tree, then go deep per method.

Method selection decision tree

Can you randomize at the individual level without interference?
Yes

No — why not?
Units interfere (marketplace, network, shared inventory)?

1 · Difference-in-Differences (DiD)parallel trends

Idea. Compare the change over time in a treated group to the change in an untreated comparison group; the comparison group's change estimates what would have happened to the treated group anyway.

DiD = (Y_treat,post − Y_treat,pre) − (Y_ctrl,post − Y_ctrl,pre) Regression: Y = β₀ + β₁·Treated + β₂·Post + β₃·(Treated×Post) + ε ; β₃ = effect

Key assumption — parallel trends: absent treatment, both groups would have moved in parallel. Untestable directly; support it by plotting multiple pre-periods (event-study plot: leads should be ≈ 0), placebo tests on fake treatment dates, and arguing no contemporaneous shocks hit one group only.

Advantages: differencing removes all time-invariant group differences AND all common shocks. Simple, transparent, defensible.

Limitations: differential trends break it (Ashenfelter dip: units treated BECAUSE they were declining); staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects biases two-way fixed effects (use Callaway–Sant'Anna or Sun–Abraham estimators); SEs must be clustered at the group level.

AirbnbA city imposes a 90-night annual cap. Effect on supply? Compare listing counts in the capped city before/after vs. similar uncapped cities. Event-study plot of monthly leads/lags makes the parallel-trends case visually.
2 · Synthetic Controlweighted twin

Idea. When one aggregate unit (a city, a country) is treated, build its counterfactual as a weighted combination of untreated units that best reproduces its pre-treatment trajectory. Weights ≥ 0, sum to 1, chosen to minimize pre-period prediction error.

Construction. 1) Donor pool of unaffected units. 2) Fit weights on pre-period outcomes (and predictors). 3) Counterfactual = weighted donor outcomes post-treatment. 4) Effect = actual − synthetic. 5) Inference via placebo runs: apply the method to every donor; the treated unit's gap should be extreme in that distribution (permutation p-value).

Use cases: single-market launches, policy shocks, PR events — anywhere n_treated = 1 kills DiD's averaging.

AirbnbAirbnb launches "Experiences" only in Paris. Synthetic Paris = 0.4·London + 0.3·Rome + 0.2·Barcelona + 0.1·Amsterdam, matched on 18 months of pre-launch bookings, seasonality, and traveler mix. Post-launch gap = causal estimate of Experiences on total bookings.

Limitations: needs a good donor pool and long pre-period; fails if pre-fit is poor; interference (Paris launch pulls tourists from London) contaminates donors.

3 · Regression Discontinuity (RDD)cutoff

Idea. When treatment is assigned by a threshold on a running variable, units just above and just below the cutoff are as-good-as-randomly different in treatment but nearly identical in everything else.

Sharp RDD: τ = lim(x↓c) E[Y|X=x] − lim(x↑c) E[Y|X=x] Fuzzy RDD: τ = (jump in Y at c) / (jump in P(treated) at c) — an IV at the cutoff

Sharp vs fuzzy. Sharp: crossing the cutoff fully determines treatment (rating ≥ 4.8 → Superhost badge). Fuzzy: the cutoff only shifts the probability of treatment (eligible hosts may not enroll) — scale by the first-stage jump.

Assumptions & checks. No precise manipulation of the running variable (McCrery density test for bunching at the cutoff); covariates smooth through the cutoff; results robust to bandwidth choice (local linear regression, varying windows).

AirbnbSuperhost requires rating ≥ 4.8. Compare hosts at 4.79 vs 4.81 on subsequent bookings → causal value of the badge. Check for bunching just above 4.8 (review solicitation gaming).

Limitation: identifies the effect only at the cutoff (local), and power is limited to the bandwidth's observations.

4 · Instrumental Variables (IV)LATE

Problem solved — endogeneity: treatment is correlated with unobservables that also drive the outcome (self-selection, omitted variables, reverse causality, measurement error).

A valid instrument Z must satisfy: (1) Relevance — Z moves treatment (testable: strong first stage, F > 10); (2) Exclusion — Z affects Y only through T (untestable, argued); (3) Independence — Z is as-good-as-random w.r.t. potential outcomes.

Wald/2SLS: τ = Cov(Z,Y)/Cov(Z,T) = (effect of Z on Y)/(effect of Z on T)

LATE. IV identifies the Local Average Treatment Effect — the effect on compliers, the units whose treatment status the instrument changed. Not the population ATE; always say who the compliers are.

AirbnbDoes using Smart Pricing raise host revenue? Adoption is self-selected. Instrument: randomized encouragement — an email nudging a random subset of hosts to enable it. Email → adoption (+8pp first stage); IV = ITT effect on revenue ÷ 0.08 = effect among hosts who adopted because of the email.

Limitation: weak instruments → huge variance and bias toward OLS; exclusion violations are silent killers (did the email also remind hosts to update their calendar?).

5 · Propensity Score Matching (PSM)ignorability

Idea. Model each unit's probability of treatment given observed covariates, e(x) = P(T=1|X); compare treated and untreated units with similar scores. Balancing on the scalar e(x) balances all of X (Rosenbaum–Rubin).

Process. 1) Fit propensity model (logit/GBM) on pre-treatment covariates only. 2) Check overlap/common support; trim extremes. 3) Match (1:1 nearest neighbor with caliper ≈ 0.2 SD of logit), or weight (IPW), or stratify. 4) Verify balance: standardized mean differences < 0.1 on all covariates. 5) Estimate effect on matched sample; sensitivity analysis (Rosenbaum bounds) for hidden bias.

The assumption that does all the work — conditional ignorability: NO unobserved confounders given X. Matching fixes comparison mechanics, never omitted-variable bias. Doubly-robust estimators (AIPW) combine the propensity model with an outcome model: consistent if either is right.

AirbnbHosts who add professional photos earn more — causal? Match photo-adopters to non-adopters on market, listing type, capacity, price tier, tenure, prior bookings, response rate. Remaining gap is the estimate — under the assumption no unobserved "host ambition" drives both. Better: pair with the historical randomized photography-offer experiment for calibration.
6 · Causal Impact (Bayesian structural time series)Google

Idea. Google's CausalImpact builds a Bayesian structural time-series model of the treated series using control series (unaffected markets, related metrics) as predictors, forecasts the post-period counterfactual, and reports the gap with credible intervals.

vs synthetic control: handles seasonality and local trends natively, gives full posterior uncertainty, allows many predictor series with spike-and-slab selection; but assumes controls are unaffected by the intervention and the pre-period relationship persists.

AirbnbA Super Bowl TV campaign airs nationally in the US. Model US daily signups from CA/UK/AU signups + search-trend covariates over 12 pre-months; post-campaign gap = incremental signups, with a credible interval that honestly widens over time.
7 · Interrupted Time Series (ITS)segmented regression
Y_t = β₀ + β₁·t + β₂·Post_t + β₃·(t−t₀)·Post_t + ε_t β₂ = level change, β₃ = slope change

No control group — the pre-trend itself is the counterfactual. Demands: stable pre-trend, sharp intervention date, no co-occurring events, seasonality modeled, autocorrelation-robust SEs (Newey–West). The weakest quasi-experimental design; strengthen with a control series whenever one exists (→ becomes DiD/CausalImpact).

AirbnbA mandatory app-store policy change hits all iOS users on one date. Segmented regression on 26 weeks of iOS booking conversion, with Android as a strengthening control series.
8 · Geo Experimentsregion-randomized

Idea. Randomize regions (cities, DMAs) instead of users — the standard for marketing/brand spend where individual exposure isn't controllable, and for features with strong within-market interference.

Design. Pair or stratify markets on size/trend; randomize within pairs; analyze with DiD, trimmed-match, or time-based regression (Google's GeoX/Trimmed Match). Power is the binding constraint — 30–100 geos is typical, so effects must be large or designs matched tightly. Spillovers across geo borders (commuters, shared media markets) need buffer zones.

AirbnbMeasure TV + billboard incrementality: 40 matched US DMA pairs, treat one per pair for 8 weeks, DiD on first bookings. Result feeds the marketing-mix model's calibration.
9 · Switchback Experimentstime-sliced

Idea. Alternate the whole market (or each region) between treatment and control across time windows (e.g., 30–120 min blocks), randomized order. Each window experiences one coherent world — interference through shared supply is contained within windows.

Where it rules: Uber/Lyft pricing & dispatch, DoorDash assignment algorithms, any logistics lever where rider-driver coupling makes user-randomization lie (treatment riders grab cars that control riders would have gotten → control is harmed → effect overstated).

Design decisions. Window length: long enough for the system to re-equilibrate (carryover decays), short enough for many windows (power). Randomize in blocks balanced over hour-of-day/day-of-week. Analysis: window-level means with HAC/cluster-robust SEs; burn-in periods at switches discard transition contamination.

UberTesting a new surge algorithm: 60-minute switchbacks per city over 3 weeks, first 10 minutes after each switch discarded, city-week clustered SEs. Airbnb analog: same-day pricing recommendations in a market.
10 · Staggered Rollouts / Stepped Wedgewaves

Idea. When everyone must eventually get the feature, randomize the order in which markets/cohorts receive it. Later waves serve as controls for earlier ones; every unit eventually contributes treated and untreated periods.

Analysis caution. Naïve two-way fixed effects is biased when effects vary over time/cohort (already-treated units become bad controls). Use modern event-study estimators (Callaway–Sant'Anna). Randomize the wave order — convenience-ordered rollouts (biggest markets first) reintroduce confounding.

AirbnbNew host onboarding flow shipped to 12 country groups over 6 biweekly waves in randomized order; effect estimated from wave-vs-not-yet-treated comparisons.
11 · Natural Experimentsfound randomness

Idea. Exploit assignment variation you didn't create but that is plausibly as-good-as-random: policy changes, lotteries, outages, weather, arbitrary administrative boundaries.

ExamplesA 2-hour search outage hitting one datacenter's traffic → causal effect of search availability on weekly retention. A city's sudden short-term-rental regulation → supply elasticity (via DiD vs. unaffected neighbors). Visa-rule changes shifting travel corridors → demand substitution patterns.

Discipline: the "naturalness" must be argued — who was affected and was it correlated with outcomes? Usually analyzed with DiD/RDD/IV machinery on top of the natural variation.

12 · Holdout Groupslong-term truth

Idea. Keep a small random slice (1–5%) of users out of a feature, a model, or an entire program (all marketing emails; the whole recommendation system) for months. Measures cumulative impact, catches novelty decay, and prices the whole portfolio rather than each increment.

AirbnbA 2% global holdout from ALL CRM/push messaging for two quarters answers "what is the entire lifecycle-marketing program worth?" — routinely revealing that many individually-significant emails sum to far less than their individual readouts claimed (cannibalization across messages).

Costs: revenue sacrifice, experience inconsistency, eroding validity as the holdout's world diverges (network effects leak), organizational pressure to raid it.

13 · Shadow Testingsilent parallel

Idea. Run the new system in parallel with production, scoring real traffic, but never exposing its outputs. Compare predictions, latency, coverage, and decision deltas offline before any user-facing risk.

AirbnbA new fraud model shadow-scores all bookings for 4 weeks: agreement matrix vs. incumbent, predicted block-rate by segment, precision on label-matured cases. Catches calibration and ops-load issues before a single legitimate guest is blocked. Limit: cannot measure feedback effects of actually acting (blocked fraudsters don't adapt to a shadow).
14 · Ghost Experiments / Counterfactual Loggingwould-have

Idea. Log what the system would have done under the alternative policy, without doing it — e.g., for users in control, record that the new module "would have shown listing X at rank 2." Enables triggered analysis with identical trigger definitions in both arms, and off-policy evaluation of ranking/ads systems (counterfactual estimators like inverse propensity scoring on logged exploration data).

AirbnbTesting a "flexible dates" suggestion shown only when the model fires: counterfactual-log the firing decision in control → analyze exactly the would-have-been-exposed users in both arms → undiluted effect with no selection bias.
15 · Before–After Analysishandle with care

What it is. Compare the metric after launch to before. It is not a causal method — it attributes every seasonal swing, trend, marketing burst, and concurrent launch to your feature.

Acceptable when: the effect is enormous relative to background variation (10× a stable baseline), the metric is otherwise dead-flat, no co-occurring changes, and the decision is reversible and cheap. Even then, prefer ITS with modeled trend/seasonality.

Dangerous when: seasonal businesses (all of travel), gradual rollouts, any concurrent change, mean-reversion after launching because a metric dipped (regression to the mean masquerades as impact).

Interview red flag"Bookings rose 6% after we launched in March" — March beats February every year in travel. Saying this uncritically fails the screen; recognizing regression-to-the-mean and seasonality is the table stakes answer.
Section 13

Marketplace Experimentation

Two-sided marketplaces break the assumptions user-level A/B testing relies on. Here's how Airbnb-class companies measure anyway.

Why marketplaces are different

Guests and hosts share one inventory pool. When treatment guests book a listing, it disappears for control guests — the arms compete. This violates SUTVA (Section 14) and typically overstates demand-side wins: treatment "steals" bookings rather than creating them. The reverse happens on the supply side: activating treatment hosts adds inventory that control-side guests also enjoy, understating effects.

Canonical exampleA ranking change makes treatment guests book sooner. In the test: +3% conversion. At 100% launch: +0.5% — most of the test "lift" was inventory cannibalized from control guests. The constraint was supply all along.

The marketplace metric stack

Liquidity metrics

  • Search-to-book rate (demand-side liquidity)
  • % searches with ≥ N relevant available listings
  • Booking lead time distribution
  • Occupancy rate by market (supply utilization)
  • Unfulfilled search rate / zero-result rate

Matching & health metrics

  • Match quality: review score of completed stays, rebooking rate
  • Host acceptance rate & response time
  • Cancellation rate (both sides)
  • Supply growth: new active listings, retention of hosts
  • Price dispersion & fill-rate balance across segments

Reading rule: demand experiments must watch supply guardrails and vice versa. A guest-promo win with rising host cancellations or compressed acceptance rates is borrowing from marketplace health.

Direct vs. indirect effects

Total effect = Direct effect (on treated users' behavior) + Indirect/equilibrium effect (through shared inventory, prices, ranking feedback) User-split A/B measures: Direct + (Treatment−Control interference) ≠ Total at 100%

Coupon users book more — but would they have booked anyway (deadweight)? And did they take inventory from full-price guests (cannibalization)? Measure incrementality with the coupon as a randomized instrument and check market-level GBV in a geo design; net revenue = incremental bookings × margin − coupon cost − displaced full-price margin.

Host incentive (new-listing bonus)supply growth

Cannot randomize a publicly-advertised bonus within a market (hosts talk; fairness). Geo-randomize the program across matched market pairs; watch quality guardrails (new-listing review scores, cancellation rates) because paid supply can be low-intent supply.

Section 14

Network Effects & Interference

When one user's treatment changes another user's outcome, the control group stops being a counterfactual — and standard A/B results can be wrong in size and even sign.

SUTVA — the assumption everything rests on

The Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption: each unit's outcome depends only on its OWN treatment status (no interference), and there's one well-defined version of treatment. Every t-test on an A/B readout silently assumes it.

How it breaks

MechanismExampleBias direction (typical)
Shared inventoryAirbnb listings, Uber drivers, ad budgetsDemand-side wins overstated (cannibalization)
Direct network valueFacebook/LinkedIn messaging features: treated users message control friends who lack the featureUnderstated (value leaks into control, or feature underperforms with mixed networks)
Viral/spillover featuresReferral programs, shared wishlists — control users get referred/invitedUnderstated (contaminated control)
Algorithmic feedbackTreatment behavior changes the trained ranking model serving everyoneUnpredictable
Marketplace equilibriumTreatment-driven price/wage shifts affect all participantsUnpredictable
Company casesUber: rider-side promo tests overstated lift until switchbacks revealed driver supply was the binding constraint. LinkedIn: ego-cluster experiments (randomize a member plus their network neighborhood) to measure feed/messaging features whose value depends on peers having them. Facebook: Gboard-style cluster designs and "network bucket" tests for sharing features; viral features measured with cluster + holdout combinations. Airbnb: ranking and pricing tested with market-level designs once guest-level tests proved inflated.

Detection

  • Dose-response by exposure: does a control user's outcome correlate with the treated fraction of their market/network neighborhood?
  • Constraint segmentation: compare lift in supply-constrained vs. slack markets.
  • Scale-up discrepancy: 50% test lift ≫ realized 100% launch lift is the post-mortem signature.
  • Design-based: run user-randomized and cluster-randomized versions concurrently; the gap estimates interference.

Remedies

  • Cluster randomization at the level that contains the interference (market, social cluster, ego-network) + cluster-robust inference.
  • Switchbacks for fast-mixing systems.
  • Two-sided / multi-cell designs to estimate direct and spillover components separately.
  • Exposure modeling (analyze by realized exposure to treated neighbors) when clusters are impossible.

Section 15

Advanced Product Analytics

The analytical toolkit around experimentation: funnels, cohorts, LTV, attribution, incrementality, uplift, and evaluating ranking systems.

Funnel analysisSearch → View → Book

Decompose conversion into stages: Search → Listing view → Booking request → Acceptance → Completed stay. Compute stage conversion, identify the bleeding step, segment it (platform, market, intent), then hypothesize and test fixes. Rules of craft: define windows per stage (a booking 30 days after search still counts?); dedupe at user level vs. event level deliberately; beware mix shifts — an improvement in top-funnel quality changes the population reaching lower stages (a Simpson trap in funnel form).

Cohort & retention analysistriangles

Group users by start period; track activity at week/month N since start. The retention triangle separates aging effects (everyone decays with tenure) from cohort effects (product got better) from period effects (seasonality, shocks). For travel, define retention on realistic cycles — annual rebooking matters more than weekly opens; unbounded vs. bounded retention definitions change the story. A heatmap is in Section 17's spirit: read down columns for product improvement, across rows for lifecycle decay.

Segmentationactionable cuts

Good segments are mutually exclusive, durable, sized for power, and connected to a lever: new vs. returning, business vs. leisure (inferable from lead time, weekday pattern, party size), market tier, host professional vs. individual. Behavioral clustering (k-means on RFM-style features) is a hypothesis generator; validate that clusters differ on FUTURE behavior, not just descriptors.

LTVdiscounted margin
LTV = Σ_t m_t · r_t / (1+d)^t (margin × retention, discounted) Simple form: LTV ≈ ARPU × margin × 1/churn

For Airbnb-style infrequent purchase: model P(rebooking within k months) and expected booking value via survival or gradient-boosted models on early-trip signals. LTV:CAC by acquisition channel sets marketing ceilings; LTV deltas convert experiment retention lifts into dollars.

Churn analysisdefinition first

In non-subscription products churn is a definition choice: e.g., "no booking in 18 months". Use survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier curves by cohort/segment; Cox models for drivers) rather than fixed-window snapshots. Distinguish leading indicators (search frequency decay, app deletions) from the lagging definition; intervene on leaders, measure on the definition — with a randomized winback test, since churn-predicted users regress to the mean and uplift, not risk, decides targeting.

Attributioncredit assignment

Rules-based (last-touch, first-touch, position-based, time-decay) are accounting conventions, not causal truths; data-driven (Shapley, Markov removal-effect) are better conventions, still not causal. The reconciliation: use attribution for steady-state budget bookkeeping, and calibrate with periodic incrementality experiments (geo tests, holdouts) per channel. Branded search famously claims credit attribution gives it but incrementality tests strip away.

Incrementalitythe causal question
Incrementality = (conversions with program − conversions without) / conversions with program Measured by: holdouts, geo experiments, ghost ads / PSA tests, intent-to-treat designs

The universal question behind promos, ads, notifications, and ML systems: what happened ONLY because of this? Deadweight (would-have-converted-anyway) is the default explanation for most observational "wins".

Uplift modelingCATE targeting

Predict each user's treatment effect, not their outcome: target persuadables, skip sure-things and lost-causes, and never poke the sleeping dogs (negative-effect users — e.g., a discount reminding a subscriber to cancel). Trained on randomized data via meta-learners (T/S/X/R-learner), causal forests, or class-transformation. Evaluate with Qini/uplift curves — AUC on outcomes is the wrong metric. Production loop: experiment → train uplift → target → re-experiment to verify policy gain.

Recommendation & search evaluationoffline + online

Offline: Precision@k, Recall@k, NDCG (graded, position-discounted), MRR, coverage/diversity/novelty. Offline gains routinely fail online — feedback loops, position bias in logged data (correct with IPS on exploration traffic).

DCG@k = Σ (2^relᵢ − 1)/log₂(i+1) ; NDCG = DCG/IDCG

Online (search quality): search success rate (click within top-k), abandonment rate, time-to-first-click, reformulation rate, booking conversion per search, zero-result rate. Tie ranking experiments to booking outcomes, not clicks alone (clickbait listings book worse — Goodhart again).

Section 18

Interview Question Bank — 75 Questions

Every question with the thought process interviewers want to hear, an ideal answer, and the mistakes that sink candidates. Filter by category.

Q1 · Explain p-value to a non-technical PM.stats
Thought process

Tests communication. Avoid jargon; use the "assume nothing happened" frame; give a one-sentence version plus what it is NOT.

Ideal answer

"If the feature truly did nothing, the p-value is the chance we'd still see a difference this large just from random noise. p = 0.03 means: in a world where the feature is useless, only 3% of experiments would look this good by luck — so luck is an unlikely explanation." Then add: it is not the probability the feature works, and a small p doesn't mean the effect is big.

Common mistakes

Saying "probability the null is true" or "95% chance the result is real"; drowning the PM in sampling-distribution theory.

Q2 · What's the difference between Type I and Type II errors, and which is worse?stats
Thought process

Definitions are table stakes; "which is worse" tests business judgment — the answer is contextual cost asymmetry.

Ideal answer

Type I: shipping a false win (cost: wasted roadmap, possible harm, polluted learning). Type II: killing a real win (cost: forgone value). Which is worse depends on reversibility and costs: for an irreversible pricing change, Type I dominates → lower α; for cheap UI iterations, Type II dominates → favor power and velocity. State the trade explicitly rather than picking one universally.

Common mistakes

Treating α = 0.05 / power = 0.80 as laws rather than chosen trade-offs; not connecting errors to dollar costs.

Q3 · Your metric has extreme outliers (a $50k booking). How do you test it?stats
Thought process

Heavy tails inflate variance and break t-test reliability at realistic n. Enumerate options with trade-offs and stress pre-registration.

Ideal answer

Options: (1) winsorize/cap at a pre-registered percentile (p99.9) — changes the estimand slightly, massive variance gain; (2) analyze a binarized or quantile version alongside the mean; (3) bootstrap CIs; (4) log-transform with care (effect becomes multiplicative, zeros need handling); (5) trimmed means. I'd pre-register a capped primary metric and report the uncapped mean as secondary, plus check whether the outlier landed in one arm by chance.

Common mistakes

Deleting outliers after seeing the result (p-hacking); log-transforming and then interpreting the effect as if on raw dollars.

Q4 · You run 20 metrics in an experiment. Three are significant. Now what?stats
Thought process

Multiple testing. Expected false positives at α=0.05 over 20 tests ≈ 1; three hits is more than chance but each is suspect.

Ideal answer

First, the decision should ride on the pre-registered primary metric. For the other hits: under 20 nulls you'd expect ~1 significant by chance, so apply BH/FDR to see which survive, check whether the significant metrics are correlated (one underlying movement, not three), and whether their directions form a coherent causal story. Treat survivors as hypotheses for a follow-up if they'd change the decision.

Common mistakes

Cherry-picking the three as the narrative; applying Bonferroni so harshly that the analysis says nothing; ignoring metric correlation.

Q5 · Explain confidence intervals vs. credible intervals.stats
Thought process

Frequentist vs Bayesian interpretation — a favorite senior-screen question because almost everyone states the CI wrong.

Ideal answer

A 95% confidence interval is a statement about the procedure: across repeated experiments, 95% of such intervals contain the truth — for any single interval, the truth is either in it or not. A 95% credible interval is a statement about the parameter: given the data and a prior, there's a 95% posterior probability the parameter lies inside. Stakeholders interpret every interval the Bayesian way; in practice the numbers often nearly coincide with flat priors, which is why the abuse is tolerated.

Common mistakes

"95% probability the true effect is in the CI" stated as frequentist fact; claiming the two are always numerically identical.

Q6 · When would you use a non-parametric test?stats
Thought process

Show you know what they buy (no distributional form) and what they cost (testing a different hypothesis, less power when parametric assumptions hold).

Ideal answer

Mann–Whitney/rank tests when n is small and the metric is heavily skewed or ordinal, or when I care about stochastic dominance rather than means. Caveats: at internet scale CLT usually rescues the t-test for means; Mann–Whitney tests "P(X > Y) ≠ ½", not equal means — under unequal variances it can reject even with equal means/medians. For business decisions denominated in money, the mean (with winsorizing/bootstrap) is usually the right target anyway.

Common mistakes

"Use Mann–Whitney to compare medians" (it doesn't, in general); reflexively going non-parametric at n = 1M.

Q7 · Bookings dropped 3% week-over-week. Is it real?stats
Thought process

Anomaly triage: variance baseline → seasonality → segmentation → data quality → external events. Structure beats statistics here.

Ideal answer

First quantify normal WoW volatility (is 3% within historical noise bands?) and seasonal pattern (holiday, school calendar, YoY same-week). Then check data quality: logging changes, pipeline lag, bot filters. Then decompose: by market, platform, funnel stage, new/returning — a uniform drop suggests measurement or macro; a concentrated drop points to a launch or local event. Cross-check an independent data source (payments vs events). Only then form causal hypotheses and look for matching changes (releases, pricing, competitor moves).

Common mistakes

Jumping to a causal story before ruling out instrumentation; treating any WoW move as signal without a noise baseline.

Q8 · What is statistical power and what increases it?stats
Thought process

Definition + the four levers + the practical lever everyone forgets (variance reduction).

Ideal answer

Power is the probability of detecting an effect of a given size when it truly exists (1 − β). It rises with sample size, with larger true effects, with higher α, and with lower metric variance. The practical levers when traffic is fixed: CUPED/covariate adjustment, winsorization, triggered analysis, a more sensitive proxy metric, longer duration in whole weeks, and bolder treatments. The shortcut n ≈ 16σ²/δ² makes the trades explicit.

Common mistakes

Computing post-hoc power from the observed effect; forgetting that unequal splits (90/10) cost power vs 50/50.

Q9 · Why might a result be statistically significant but not practically significant?stats
Thought process

Separate p-values (evidence against null) from effect size (business value). Mention the huge-n regime and decision thresholds.

Ideal answer

With millions of users, even a +0.02% lift reaches p < 0.001 — real but possibly below maintenance cost, added complexity, or opportunity cost of the roadmap slot. The fix is deciding on the CI against a pre-specified practical threshold: ship if the interval clears the minimum effect that justifies the launch. Conversely, p = 0.08 on a large estimated lift in an underpowered test may justify extending, not killing.

Common mistakes

Using p-value size as effect size ("p = 0.0001, huge win!"); having no pre-specified practical threshold at all.

Q10 · A/A test shows significance 15% of the time at α = 0.05. Diagnose.stats
Thought process

Variance underestimation. Lead with the unit-of-analysis mismatch — the most common industrial cause.

Ideal answer

The false-positive rate is 3× nominal → variance is understated or assignment is broken. Suspects in order: (1) analysis unit finer than randomization unit (page views analyzed, users randomized → correlated observations; fix with delta method/user-level aggregation); (2) non-independent assignment (hash collisions, carryover from a previous experiment on the same hash); (3) peeking baked into the "result"; (4) metric outliers making normal approximations fail. A/A simulation with corrected variance should restore ~5%.

Common mistakes

Shrugging it off as bad luck; "fixing" by raising the significance bar instead of repairing variance estimation.

Q11 · Design an experiment: Airbnb wants to show total price upfront.experimentation
Thought process

Use the 8-step framework: clarify → hypothesis → metrics → unit/power → risks (host gaming, market heterogeneity) → decision rule.

Ideal answer

Clarify scope (markets, platforms, regulatory constraints). Hypothesis: transparency cuts checkout abandonment, possibly cuts listing CTR; net booking conversion neutral-to-up. Primary: bookings per searcher; secondary: abandonment, CTR, AOV; guardrails: cancellations, support contacts, host fee-restructuring behavior. Guest-randomized, search-triggered, 2 weeks by power calc, market segments pre-registered. Key risk: host-side strategic response (shifting fees) operates on a slower clock than the test — propose a long-term holdout or staggered geo confirmation. Decision rule pre-committed: ship if conversion not worse than −0.2% and abandonment improves.

Common mistakes

No clarifying questions; forgetting the supply side reacts; declaring CTR drop a failure when it's an expected mechanism.

Q12 · Your experiment shows +2% on the primary metric, p = 0.04, but a guardrail dropped. Ship?experimentation

Q13 · How long should an experiment run?experimentation

Q14 · What is SRM and what causes it?experimentation
Thought process

Define, give the test, list causes by pipeline stage, and state the discipline: SRM = stop.

Ideal answer

Sample Ratio Mismatch: the realized split deviates from the configured split beyond chance (chi-square p < 0.001 on assignment counts). Causes: assignment bugs (hashing, race conditions), differential logging (treatment crashes drop events), redirects losing users in one arm, bot filtering interacting with the variant, pipeline joins dropping arms unevenly. Because the missing users are almost never random, ANY analysis on SRM-affected data is biased — the answer is debug and rerun, not analyze harder.

Common mistakes

Eyeballing 50.4/49.6 at huge n as "close enough" (it can be wildly significant); trying to weight or impute around SRM.

Q15 · The treatment effect shrinks every week of the experiment. Interpret.experimentation

Ideal answer

Three candidate explanations: (1) novelty decay — diagnose by slicing the effect by user tenure-in-experiment, not calendar week; (2) population mix shift — early traffic skews heavy users, later weeks add lighter users with smaller effects; (3) mechanical dilution if the metric is cumulative. If tenure-sliced effects show new-to-treatment users responding while long-exposed users regress to zero, it's novelty → forecast long-run impact from the stabilized cohort effect or run a long-term holdout.

Common mistakes

Averaging over the whole window and shipping the inflated number; reading calendar-time decay as proof of novelty without the cohort cut.

Q16 · Explain CUPED to a senior stakeholder in two minutes.experimentation
Thought process

Analogy first, one formula max, then the business payoff (faster/cheaper tests) and the catch (new users).

Ideal answer

"Users differ hugely in baseline behavior — frequent bookers vs one-timers — and that baseline noise drowns small treatment effects. Q22 · When is a 90/10 split better than 50/50?experimentation

Q27 · Explain confounders, mediators, and colliders — and what to adjust for.causal
Thought process

The three DAG primitives + backdoor criterion; one concrete example each.

Ideal answer

Confounder (T←C→Y): common cause creating spurious association — ADJUST (host quality confounds Instant Book vs bookings). Mediator (T→M→Y): transmits the effect — DON'T adjust if you want the total effect (adjusting for "search results clicked" when measuring ranking → bookings throws away the mechanism). Collider (T→K←Y): common effect — NEVER adjust; conditioning on it manufactures association (analyzing "among first-page listings" induces fake price-quality trade-offs). The backdoor criterion formalizes it: block all backdoor paths, touch no descendants of treatment.

Common mistakes

"Control for everything available" — which conditions on colliders and mediators; defining collider bias as just selection bias without the mechanism.

Q28 · Walk me through DiD and its key assumption. How do you support it?causal
Thought process

Formula, parallel trends, and the three supports: event-study leads, placebos, no differential shocks.

Ideal answer

DiD = (treated post − pre) − (control post − pre); the control's change estimates the treated group's counterfactual change. Identification rests on parallel trends — untestable, but supported by: flat event-study leads across multiple pre-periods, placebo tests on fake intervention dates and unaffected outcomes, and an argument that no shock hit one group alone. Modern caveat: with staggered adoption and heterogeneous effects, plain two-way fixed effects is biased — use Callaway–Sant'Anna-style estimators. Cluster SEs at the assignment-group level.

Common mistakes

"Parallel pre-trends prove the assumption" (they support, not prove); units selected into treatment because of a dip (Ashenfelter) — mean reversion masquerades as effect.

Q29 · Airbnb launched referrals everywhere at once. Measure the impact.causal

Present a range, not a point.

Common mistakes

Counting all referred signups as incremental; pure before–after in a seasonal business.

Q30 · What makes a valid instrument? Give a tech example.causal
Thought process

Relevance, exclusion, independence — and that only relevance is testable. Randomized encouragement is the clean tech example.

Ideal answer

Z must (1) move treatment (testable — first-stage F > 10), (2) affect the outcome ONLY through treatment (exclusion — untestable, argued), (3) be as-good-as-random (independence). Tech's cleanest instrument is a randomized encouragement: a random nudge to adopt a feature instruments for adoption, and IV = ITT ÷ first-stage. Crucially, IV identifies the LATE — the effect on compliers, those whom the nudge moved — which may differ from the population effect.

Common mistakes

Proposing instruments with obvious exclusion violations (e.g., using "received any email" which also reminds users the product exists); reporting LATE as ATE.

Q31 · Superhost requires 4.8★. How do you measure the badge's causal value?causal

Textbook RDD: sharp threshold, local comparison, manipulation check, local-effect caveat.

Ideal answer

Regression discontinuity at the 4.8 cutoff: hosts at 4.79 vs 4.81 are nearly identical except the badge, so the outcome jump at the threshold is the badge's causal effect (locally). Implementation: local linear regression within a bandwidth, robustness across bandwidths, McCrary density test for bunching just above 4.8 (review-gaming would invalidate), covariate smoothness checks. Comparing all Superhosts vs all non-Superhosts (massive confounding); skipping the manipulation check in a system hosts actively game.

Q32 · Propensity matching showed your feature works. The VP asks: is this as good as an A/B test?causal

Q33 · Synthetic control vs DiD — when each?causal
Thought process

n-treated, parallel-trends plausibility, pre-period length, inference style.

Ideal answer

DiD when you have multiple treated and control units and parallel trends are plausible — simple, transparent, standard errors conventional. Synthetic control when ONE (or few) aggregate unit is treated and no single control matches it: the weighted donor combination manufactures a parallel-trending twin, with inference via placebo permutations. SC needs a long, well-fit pre-period and a clean donor pool (donors unaffected by the treatment); if SC weights can't reproduce the pre-period, fall back and say so. Running DiD with one treated city against a hand-picked "similar" city (cherry-picking); ignoring donor contamination via spillovers.

Q34 · What is Simpson's paradox? Give a marketplace example and the resolution.causal
Thought process

Definition, concrete numbers, and the non-mechanical resolution (causal structure decides the right level).

Ideal answer

Subgroup associations reverse on pooling when a lurking variable correlates with group composition. Example: a checkout change wins +1% on mobile and +1% on desktop but loses −0.5% pooled, because treatment's traffic skewed to mobile, where baseline conversion is structurally lower. Resolution depends on the DAG: if platform mix is a pre-treatment confounder (assignment imbalance, triggering differences), the stratified estimates are right; if treatment itself changes the mix (it shifts users to mobile), the pooled effect is the relevant total effect. Diagnose with an SRM check per segment first.

Common mistakes

"Always trust the segmented view" as a rule; missing that the paradox in an A/B test usually signals an assignment or triggering bug.

Q35 · Measure the long-term effect of a feature whose short-term test was neutral.causal

Long-term holdouts, surrogate metrics, and the honesty that 2-week tests can't see slow effects.

Ideal answer

Keep a small randomized holdout out of the feature for 3–6 months — the only design that directly measures cumulative effects (habit formation, trust, ecosystem shifts). Complement with validated surrogates: if meta-analysis across past experiments shows that movements in (say) saved-searches predict 6-month retention under intervention, a short-term surrogate movement is evidence. Slice the existing test by tenure for primacy signals. The cost argument writes itself: a 1% holdout's forgone revenue vs. the risk of scaling a feature with slow-burn harm.

Extrapolating week-2 effects linearly to a year; using observational adopter-vs-non-adopter retention as the "long-term" answer.

Q36 · Your DiD event-study shows pre-trend leads that are NOT flat. Now what?causal

Ideal answer

Burying the event-study plot; "controlling for the trend" with one pre-period (impossible).

Q37 · ATE vs ATT vs LATE vs CATE — when does each matter?causal

Reporting LATE as "the effect"; targeting on predicted outcome instead of predicted effect (CATE).

Q38 · Why do guest-side tests overstate booking lifts at Airbnb?marketplace

Q41 · How would you measure marketplace liquidity, and why does it matter for experiments?marketplace

Q47 · Switchback windows: how do you choose their length?marketplace
Thought process

Bias-variance trade: carryover vs number of windows; empirical decay estimation; burn-in.

Ideal answer

Q48 · A two-sided test: randomize guests AND hosts. What does the 2×2 buy you?marketplace

Forgetting that mixed cells may be UX-infeasible; analyzing cells without modeling the match-level exposure.

Q50 · How do network effects bias a referral-program experiment, and how do you design around it?marketplace
Thought process

Control contamination via referrals reaching control users; cluster designs; what the contaminated estimate still tells you.

Ideal answer

If treated users can refer ANYONE, control users receive referrals — control is partially treated, biasing the ITT toward zero (and signups attributed to "treatment" double-count what control absorbed). Blocking control users from receiving referrals (creates a weird, non-launch-like world); ignoring that virality is precisely the effect of interest.

Q52 · Define and compute LTV for Airbnb guests. What makes it hard?analytics
Thought process

Infrequent-purchase LTV: long cycles, margin vs GBV, discounting, model-based prediction.

Ideal answer

LTV = Σ over horizon of expected contribution margin per period × survival probability, discounted. For travel: margin = take rate × GBV − variable costs (support, payment, incentives); cycles are annual-ish, so horizon is 3–5 years and "churn" is a soft definition (no booking in 18–24 months). Practically: a predictive model (early signals — first-trip review, app installs, saved searches — predicting future booking count/value) calibrated on mature historical cohorts. ARPU/churn formulas built for subscriptions; ignoring discounting and censoring; LTV on bookings rather than margin.

Q57 · Design an alerting system for business metrics. What makes a good alert?analytics

Q60 · Quantify the revenue impact of search latency.analytics
Thought process

Observational latency-conversion curves are confounded (network quality, geography, query complexity) — the gold answer is injected-latency experiments.

Regression on observational latency; reporting averages when the damage is in the p95+ tail; forgetting mobile/web heterogeneity.

Q61 · Should Airbnb build a loyalty program? How would you analyze it?product sense

Q69 · SQL: compute each experiment arm's conversion rate and a z-statistic.SQL
Thought process

User-level dedup first (assignment table is the spine), LEFT JOIN conversions, then the z formula in SQL.

Ideal answer
WITH users AS ( SELECT user_id, variant FROM assignments WHERE exp_id = 'exp_42' ), conv AS ( SELECT a.variant, COUNT(*) AS n, COUNT(b.user_id) AS x -- users with ≥1 booking FROM users a LEFT JOIN (SELECT DISTINCT user_id FROM bookings WHERE created_at BETWEEN exp_start AND exp_end) b USING (user_id) GROUP BY 1 ), s AS ( SELECT MAX(CASE WHEN variant='T' THEN x*1.0/n END) AS pt, MAX(CASE WHEN variant='T' THEN n END) AS nt, MAX(CASE WHEN variant='C' THEN x*1.0/n END) AS pc, MAX(CASE WHEN variant='C' THEN n END) AS nc, SUM(x)*1.0/SUM(n) AS pp FROM conv ) SELECT pt, pc, pt-pc AS lift, (pt-pc)/SQRT(pp*(1-pp)*(1.0/nt+1.0/nc)) AS z FROM s;

Key craft: DISTINCT user in conversions (analysis unit = randomization unit), assignment table as the denominator spine (LEFT JOIN preserves non-converters), conversion window bounded to the experiment period.

Common mistakes

INNER JOIN dropping non-converters; counting bookings instead of converting users; unbounded conversion windows.

Q70 · SQL: detect SRM for all running experiments.SQL
Thought process

Compare observed arm counts to expected split; chi-square statistic per experiment; flag extreme.

Ideal answer
WITH counts AS ( SELECT exp_id, variant, COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS n FROM assignments GROUP BY 1,2 ), tot AS ( SELECT exp_id, SUM(n) AS total FROM counts GROUP BY 1 ), expected AS ( -- expected_share from config table SELECT c.exp_id, c.variant, c.n, t.total * e.expected_share AS exp_n FROM counts c JOIN tot t USING (exp_id) JOIN exp_config e USING (exp_id, variant) ) SELECT exp_id, SUM(POWER(n - exp_n, 2) / exp_n) AS chi_sq, COUNT(*) - 1 AS dof FROM expected GROUP BY 1 HAVING SUM(POWER(n - exp_n, 2)/exp_n) > 10.83; -- p<0.001, dof=1

Run as a scheduled job; alert owners. Mention that the 10.83 cut is for 2 arms (dof = 1) — multi-arm tests need the dof-appropriate critical value.

Common mistakes

Using a loose p<0.05 cut (false SRM alarms at fleet scale); counting events instead of distinct users.

Q71 · SQL: build day-N retention by signup cohort.SQL
Thought process

Cohort = signup date; activity joined on date diff; rates per cohort×day; mind maturity filtering.

Ideal answer
WITH cohort AS ( SELECT user_id, DATE(created_at) AS d0 FROM users ), act AS ( SELECT DISTINCT user_id, DATE(event_at) AS d FROM activity ) SELECT c.d0, DATE_DIFF(a.d, c.d0, DAY) AS day_n, COUNT(DISTINCT a.user_id) * 1.0 / COUNT(DISTINCT c.user_id) AS retention FROM cohort c LEFT JOIN act a USING (user_id) WHERE DATE_DIFF(a.d, c.d0, DAY) IN (1,7,14,28) AND c.d0 <= DATE_SUB(CURRENT_DATE, INTERVAL 28 DAY) -- mature only GROUP BY 1,2 ORDER BY 1,2;

Note the bug in the naive version: the WHERE on the LEFT JOINed table silently turns it into an INNER join for the denominator — the clean fix computes denominators separately or uses conditional aggregation: COUNT(DISTINCT IF(day_n=7, user_id, NULL)) / cohort_size. Call this out proactively.

Immature cohorts deflating recent retention; the LEFT JOIN + WHERE denominator bug; bounded vs unbounded retention confusion.

Q72 · SQL: sessionize raw events with a 30-minute inactivity gap.SQL
Thought process

Gaps-and-islands: LAG to find gaps, flag session starts, cumulative SUM to assign session IDs.

Ideal answer
WITH flagged AS ( SELECT user_id, event_at, CASE WHEN event_at - LAG(event_at) OVER w > INTERVAL '30' MINUTE OR LAG(event_at) OVER w IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS new_sess FROM events WINDOW w AS (PARTITION BY user_id ORDER BY event_at) ) SELECT user_id, event_at, SUM(new_sess) OVER (PARTITION BY user_id ORDER BY event_at) AS session_seq FROM flagged;

From here: GROUP BY user_id, session_seq for session metrics (duration, events, conversion). The pattern (LAG → flag → running SUM) is the universal gaps-and-islands template — say its name.

Self-joins instead of window functions; forgetting the NULL first-event case; per-event timezone bugs.

Q73 · SQL: pre-experiment covariate for CUPED — each user's bookings in the 28 days before THEIR assignment.SQL
Thought process

Per-user anchored window (assignment time differs per user) — join bookings on a user-relative interval, not a fixed date.

Ideal answer
SELECT a.user_id, a.variant, COALESCE(COUNT(b.booking_id), 0) AS pre_bookings FROM assignments a LEFT JOIN bookings b ON b.user_id = a.user_id AND b.created_at >= a.assigned_at - INTERVAL '28' DAY AND b.created_at < a.assigned_at -- strictly pre-exposure GROUP BY 1, 2;

The strict inequality at assignment time is the statistical point: any post-assignment leakage into the covariate biases CUPED. New users get 0 (no reduction for them). Join this to the outcome table and compute θ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X) pooled across arms.

Fixed calendar pre-period instead of per-user anchored; including assignment-day events (leakage); dropping zero-history users instead of zero-filling.

Q74 · SQL: find listings whose booking rate is significantly below their market's average.SQL
Thought process

Window functions for market baselines; a stabilized comparison (volume-aware), not raw rate ranking.

Ideal answer

Q75 · SQL + design: an experiment's metrics pipeline double-counts users who switched devices. Find and fix.SQL
Thought process

Identity resolution meets experimentation: device-level rows for user-level randomization → dedupe, and check whether it caused SRM or contamination.

Ideal answer

Deduping without checking cross-variant contamination; excluding asymmetrically (introduces SRM); patching the query but not the pipeline.

Decision Frameworks

Compressed judgment: which test, which method, ship-or-not, and how to answer any experiment question in interviews.

Which statistical test?

What kind of metric?
Binary (converted?)
Two-proportion z-test (or chi-square). Huge n → fine; tiny n → Fisher's exact.
Continuous mean (revenue, nights)
Welch's t-test; heavy tails → winsorize / bootstrap; pre-register outlier policy.
Ratio of two user-level quantities (CTR, AOV)
Delta method variance (or user-level bootstrap) — numerator and denominator are correlated within users.
Count per user (sessions, messages)
t-test on user means usually fine at scale; Poisson/negative-binomial models if modeling rates with exposure offsets.
Percentile (p95 latency)
Quantile bootstrap or rank-based tests; never t-test a percentile directly.
Randomization unit ≠ analysis unit
Aggregate to randomization unit, or delta method / cluster-robust SEs — before anything else.

Universal interview answer pattern

  1. Clarify (goal, population, constraints) — 2–3 questions max.
  2. Structure — announce the framework you'll walk ("hypothesis → metrics → design → risks → decision").
  3. Execute with concrete numbers (baselines, MDEs, durations) — invented-but-reasonable beats vague.
  4. Attack your own design — name interference, novelty, multiple testing before they ask.
  5. Decide — end with a recommendation and what evidence would change it.

  • Confounding: common cause → adjust/randomize.
  • Selection: non-representative sample tied to T or Y.
  • Survivorship: conditioning on having survived.
  • Collider/Berkson: conditioning on a common effect manufactures association.
  • Simpson's: aggregation reverses subgroup trends.
  • Novelty/primacy: early effects ≠ steady state.
  • Winner's curse: significant underpowered results exaggerate.
  • Regression to mean: extreme baselines drift back — pre/post lies.
  • Goodhart: targeted metrics get gamed.
  • Dilution: unexposed users shrink measured effects.

6 · Marketplace experimentation

  • Shared inventory → guest-side wins overstated, supply-side effects often understated.
  • Check: lift in supply-constrained vs slack markets; test-vs-launch reconciliation.
  • Designs: cluster (markets), switchback (time), two-sided factorial, budget split.
  • Liquidity metrics: search-to-book, zero-availability %, occupancy, time-to-first-booking.
  • Always pair demand metrics with supply guardrails (acceptance, cancellations, host churn) and vice versa.
  • Switchback craft: window ≥ equilibration time, burn-in discard, hour×day blocking, cluster-robust SEs.

7 · Metric design

  • Three tiers: OEC (decides) · guardrails (veto) · diagnostics (explain).
  • Good metric: sensitive, trustworthy, directional, efficient, debuggable, ungameable.
  • Ratio metrics need delta method when randomized by user.
  • Engagement (CTR, time) = diagnostics, never OECs.
  • Validate proxies via meta-analysis against long-term holdout truth.
  • Metric tree: north star = Π funnel steps × value — every experiment claims a link.

Glossary

Forty terms, interview-precise.

A/A test
Control vs control; validates platform false-positive rates and variance estimation.
Alpha (α)
Pre-set tolerance for false positives; the significance threshold.
ATE / ATT / LATE / CATE
Average effect over: everyone / the treated / instrument-compliers / covariate-defined subgroups.
Backdoor criterion
Rule for choosing adjustment sets: block all paths into treatment, touch no descendants of it.
Bandit (multi-armed)
Adaptive allocation that shifts traffic toward winners; optimizes reward at the cost of clean inference.
Bonferroni / Holm
FWER corrections: test at α/m (Holm: stepwise, uniformly more powerful).
Carryover
Persistence of one period's treatment into the next; the switchback design's enemy.
CLT
Means of many independent draws are approximately Normal with variance σ²/n.
Cluster randomization
Assigning groups (markets, networks) to contain interference; requires cluster-robust inference.
Confidence sequence
Interval valid at every point in time simultaneously; basis of always-valid inference.
Counterfactual
The unobserved outcome under the other treatment state; what every causal method estimates.
CUPED
Variance reduction using pre-experiment covariates; Var shrinks by ρ².
Delta method
Variance approximation for functions of means; required for ratio metrics under user randomization.
DiD
Difference-in-differences; change-vs-change estimate under parallel trends.
Dilution
Effect shrinkage from including never-exposed users in analysis.
Exclusion restriction
IV assumption: the instrument affects the outcome only through treatment.
FDR / Benjamini–Hochberg
Expected share of false discoveries among declared wins; BH controls it for exploratory screens.
Fixed horizon
Analyzing only at the pre-planned end; peeking before it invalidates nominal α.
Guardrail metric
Must-not-harm metric with veto power over launches.
HTE
Heterogeneous treatment effects — effect variation across users/segments.
Holdout
Long-lived control slice measuring cumulative impact of a feature or program.
Interference
One unit's treatment affecting another's outcome; SUTVA violation.
ITT
Intent-to-treat: analyze by assignment regardless of exposure/compliance.
MDE
Minimum detectable effect at given power — the experiment's resolution.
Novelty / primacy
Transient early over/under-reaction to change.
OEC
Overall Evaluation Criterion — the pre-agreed decision metric.
Parallel trends
DiD's identifying assumption: groups would have moved together absent treatment.
Peeking
Repeated significance checks without correction; inflates false positives severalfold.
Power (1−β)
Probability of detecting a true effect of specified size.
Propensity score
P(treated | covariates); balancing scalar for observational comparisons.
p-value
P(data at least this extreme | null true). Not P(null true).
Quasi-experiment
Causal design without researcher-controlled randomization (DiD, RDD, IV, SC…).
RDD
Regression discontinuity: comparing units just across an assignment threshold.
Sequential testing
Methods (SPRT, mSPRT, OBF) keeping error rates valid under continuous monitoring.
SRM
Sample ratio mismatch — realized split deviates from configured; invalidates analysis.
SUTVA
No interference + one treatment version; the silent assumption of every t-test readout.
Switchback
Time-sliced whole-market alternation between variants.
Triggered analysis
Restricting to users meeting the (counterfactually logged) exposure condition.
Type M error
Magnitude exaggeration of significant effects in underpowered tests (winner's curse).
Winsorization
Capping extreme values at a percentile to tame variance; changes the estimand slightly.
