Section 01

What is a Confidence Interval? (Simple Intuition)

Imagine you're trying to figure out the average daily sessions per user on Google Play Games. You can't survey every user — so you take a sample of 1,000 users and compute the average.

But here's the thing: if you ran this same experiment 100 times with different random samples, you'd get 100 slightly different averages every time. A confidence interval is just a range that says: "Most of those averages would land somewhere in here."

One-liner intuition
A 95% confidence interval means: if I repeat this experiment 100 times, about 95 of those intervals will contain the true average. It's a measure of how trustworthy your estimate is, not a guarantee.

Think of it like a weather forecast. The meteorologist doesn't say "it will be exactly 28°C tomorrow." They say "between 26°C and 30°C." The CI is your range of reasonable estimates.

Section 02

When Should You Use Confidence Intervals?

  • A/B testing — Did the treatment group actually perform better, or was it just noise?
  • Estimating averages — What's the true average revenue per user, CTR, or session length?
  • Comparing two groups — Is the difference between control and treatment meaningful?
  • Survey results — "User satisfaction score is 4.2" — but how confident are we in that number?
  • Business metrics — Reporting DAU, ARPU, or retention with a reliable range, not just a point estimate.
Real-world example — Google Play Games
You launch personalised game recommendations and observe that average daily sessions increased from 3.2 to 3.4. Your manager wants to ship it. Before saying yes, you compute a 95% CI for the difference: [−0.05, +0.45]. Since the interval includes 0, the lift might just be noise. You should not ship yet.
Section 03

Core Idea — Mean, Variability & Sample Size

Three things control how wide your confidence interval is:

x̄ (mean)
Your best guess
The average from your sample. The CI is centered here. Doesn't affect width — only position.
σ (std)
How spread out the data is
High spread = wide CI. If users are very different from each other, your estimate is less reliable.
n (sample size)
How many observations
More data = tighter CI. With 10,000 users you're much more confident than with 100.
Z (confidence level)
How confident you want to be
95% → Z = 1.96. Higher confidence = wider interval. You're casting a bigger net to be more sure.
Why larger sample = tighter interval
The formula divides std by √n. So as n grows, the denominator gets bigger, making the whole margin of error smaller. 10× more data → ~3× tighter interval (√10 ≈ 3.16). This is why Google runs experiments on millions of users — it makes small effects detectable.
Section 04

The Formula (Kept Light)

CI  =  x̄  ±  Z  ×  ( σ  /  √n )

Each piece in plain English:

  • x̄ (mean) — Your sample average. The center of the interval.
  • Z — How many standard deviations wide you want to go. Depends on your confidence level.
  • σ (std) — How spread out your data is. More spread = wider interval.
  • n — Sample size. More data = smaller margin of error.
  • σ / √n — This piece is called the standard error. It's how much the sample mean can wiggle around the true mean.

Common Z-values to remember:

Confidence LevelZ-valueWhat it means
90%1.64590 out of 100 intervals will contain the true value
95% ← most common1.9695 out of 100 intervals will contain the true value
99%2.57699 out of 100 intervals will contain the true value
Section 05

Python Code — Single Sample CI

Scenario: You've collected daily session counts for 200 users. You want to estimate the true average with a 95% confidence interval.

import numpy as np from scipy import stats # --- Step 1: Generate sample data (or load your real data) --- np.random.seed(42) data = np.random.normal(loc=3.2, scale=1.5, size=200) # loc = true mean (3.2 sessions/day), scale = std, size = 200 users # --- Step 2: Compute basic stats --- n = len(data) mean = np.mean(data) std = np.std(data, ddof=1) # ddof=1 for sample std (not population) # --- Step 3: Compute 95% Confidence Interval --- confidence = 0.95 ci_low, ci_high = stats.t.interval( confidence, # confidence level df=n - 1, # degrees of freedom loc=mean, # center of interval scale=stats.sem(data) # standard error of the mean ) # --- Step 4: Print results --- print(f"Sample size : {n}") print(f"Mean : {mean:.2f}") print(f"Std Dev : {std:.2f}") print(f"95% CI : ({ci_low:.2f}, {ci_high:.2f})") # --- Expected Output --- # Sample size : 200 # Mean : 3.18 # Std Dev : 1.49 # 95% CI : (2.97, 3.39)
Why t-interval and not z-interval?
We use scipy.stats.t.interval (t-distribution) instead of z because in practice we don't know the true population std — we estimate it from the sample. The t-distribution accounts for this uncertainty. For large samples (n > 30), t and z give nearly identical results anyway.
Section 06

Python Code — Two Sample Comparison (A/B Test)

Scenario: You ran an A/B test. You want to compare control vs treatment and compute a CI for the difference in means.

import numpy as np from scipy import stats # --- Step 1: Simulate control and treatment data --- np.random.seed(42) control = np.random.normal(loc=3.2, scale=1.5, size=1000) treatment = np.random.normal(loc=3.5, scale=1.6, size=1000) # --- Step 2: Compute means and difference --- mean_control = np.mean(control) mean_treatment = np.mean(treatment) diff = mean_treatment - mean_control # --- Step 3: Compute CI for the difference using Welch's t-test --- t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(treatment, control, equal_var=False) # Manual CI for difference in means se = np.sqrt( np.var(treatment, ddof=1) / len(treatment) + np.var(control, ddof=1) / len(control) ) z = 1.96 # for 95% CI ci_low = diff - z * se ci_high = diff + z * se # --- Step 4: Print results --- print(f"Control mean : {mean_control:.3f}") print(f"Treatment mean : {mean_treatment:.3f}") print(f"Difference : {diff:.3f}") print(f"95% CI (diff) : ({ci_low:.3f}, {ci_high:.3f})") print(f"P-value : {p_value:.4f}") # --- Expected Output --- # Control mean : 3.193 # Treatment mean : 3.482 # Difference : 0.289 # 95% CI (diff) : (0.157, 0.421) # P-value : 0.0001 # --- Step 5: Decision --- if ci_low > 0: print("CI does not include 0 → statistically significant lift!") else: print("CI includes 0 → lift may just be noise. Do not ship yet.")
Why equal_var=False?
This uses Welch's t-test, which doesn't assume the two groups have equal variance. Always prefer this in real A/B tests — you rarely know if variances are equal, and Welch handles both cases safely.
Section 07

Python Code — Visualization

Two charts: a histogram showing the data distribution with CI marked, and an error bar chart comparing control vs treatment.

import numpy as np import matplotlib.pyplot as plt from scipy import stats np.random.seed(42) control = np.random.normal(3.2, 1.5, 1000) treatment = np.random.normal(3.5, 1.6, 1000) fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5)) # --- Chart 1: Histogram with CI --- mean_c = np.mean(control) ci_c = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(control)-1, loc=mean_c, scale=stats.sem(control)) ax1.hist(control, bins=40, color='steelblue', alpha=0.7, edgecolor='white') ax1.axvline(mean_c, color='black', lw=2, label=f'Mean: {mean_c:.2f}') ax1.axvline(ci_c[0], color='crimson', lw=1.5, linestyle='--', label=f'95% CI: ({ci_c[0]:.2f}, {ci_c[1]:.2f})') ax1.axvline(ci_c[1], color='crimson', lw=1.5, linestyle='--') ax1.set_title('Control: Distribution + 95% CI') ax1.set_xlabel('Daily Sessions') ax1.set_ylabel('Count') ax1.legend() # --- Chart 2: Error bars comparing control vs treatment --- groups = ['Control', 'Treatment'] means = [np.mean(control), np.mean(treatment)] errors = [1.96 * stats.sem(control), 1.96 * stats.sem(treatment)] colors = ['steelblue', 'darkorange'] ax2.bar(groups, means, color=colors, alpha=0.8, edgecolor='white', width=0.5) ax2.errorbar(groups, means, yerr=errors, fmt='none', color='black', capsize=8, lw=2) ax2.set_title('Control vs Treatment — Mean ± 95% CI') ax2.set_ylabel('Mean Daily Sessions') ax2.set_ylim(0, 4.5) plt.tight_layout() plt.savefig('ci_plot.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight') plt.show()
What to look for in the error bar chart
If the error bars of control and treatment do not overlap, the difference is likely statistically significant. If they overlap substantially, you probably have no significant effect. This is a quick visual gut-check before running the formal t-test.
Section 08

How to Interpret a CI — Very Important

This is where most people get it wrong — including in interviews. Let's fix it.

❌ Wrong interpretation
"There is a 95% probability that the true mean lies between 2.97 and 3.39."

This sounds right but it's incorrect. The true mean is fixed — it either is or isn't in this interval. Probability doesn't apply to a fixed unknown value.
✅ Correct interpretation
"If I repeated this experiment 100 times and built a CI each time, about 95 of those intervals would contain the true mean."

The randomness is in the intervals, not in the true value. This specific interval either contains the true mean or it doesn't — we just don't know which.
In a Google interview
If asked to interpret a CI, say: "This interval was constructed using a method that captures the true value 95% of the time across repeated samples. For this specific sample, the estimate is [X, Y]." This shows you understand the frequentist definition properly.

Also remember: a wider CI means less certainty (small sample or high variance). A narrower CI means more certainty. A CI that's very wide is a signal to collect more data before making decisions.

Section 09

Quick Decision Guide

The most common practical use of CI in product analytics is deciding whether an observed effect is real. Here's a simple rule:

CI includes 0 → No strong effect
Example: CI for lift = [−0.05, +0.45]. Since 0 is inside the range, the true lift might be zero. The observed difference could just be random noise. Do not ship. Collect more data.
CI excludes 0 → Likely real effect
Example: CI for lift = [+0.15, +0.42]. Since 0 is not in the range, you're 95% confident the treatment has a positive effect. Consider shipping, check practical significance too.
A/B Test example — Google Play Games
You tested personalised recommendations. Difference in avg daily sessions = +0.29. 95% CI = [+0.16, +0.42]. Since the entire interval is above 0, you're confident the feature helps. Now check: is +0.29 sessions/day meaningful for the business? Multiply by your user base and revenue per session. If yes — ship it.
Section 10

Common Mistakes to Avoid

⚠️
Small sample size
With n = 50, your CI will be very wide and unreliable. Always check if your experiment is powered enough before drawing conclusions. At Google scale, this is rarely a problem — but always verify.
⚠️
Ignoring variance
Two groups can have the same mean but wildly different spreads. Always look at std and CI width, not just the point estimate. High variance = unreliable estimate even with a large sample.
⚠️
Misinterpreting "95% probability"
The 95% refers to the method, not this specific interval. This interval either contains the true value or it doesn't — we just don't know. Don't say "there's a 95% chance the true value is in here."
⚠️
Confusing statistical and practical significance
A CI that excludes 0 means the effect is statistically significant — but is it large enough to matter? A +0.001 increase in sessions/day might be statistically significant with 10M users but completely irrelevant for the business.
⚠️
Peeking at results early
If you check your experiment every day and stop as soon as CI excludes 0, you massively inflate your false positive rate. Decide the experiment duration upfront and stick to it.
Section 11 — Bonus

Relation to P-Value

CI and p-values are two sides of the same coin. Here's the bridge:

Confidence Interval says
95% CI for the difference = [+0.16, +0.42]
→ Does not include 0
⟺
P-Value says
p-value < 0.05
→ Statistically significant
  • If 95% CI excludes 0 → p-value < 0.05 → statistically significant
  • If 95% CI includes 0 → p-value ≥ 0.05 → not statistically significant
  • CI gives you more information — it tells you the direction and magnitude of the effect, not just whether it's significant
Preferred in interviews
Always prefer reporting CI over just p-values. A CI tells your stakeholder how big the effect is and how uncertain you are. A p-value alone just says "yes/no." Google cares about business impact — so always quantify the range of plausible effects.
