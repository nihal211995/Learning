The 6-Step Mental Map

1
Identify the world states
What are the two possible truths? e.g. "Game IS a hit" vs "Game is NOT a hit". "Review IS spam" vs "Review is NOT spam".
2
Identify the Prior — P(State)
What did you believe before seeing evidence? Always comes from historical data. Never assume — ask for it. e.g. "Historically 10% of games become hits → Prior = 0.10"
3
Identify the Evidence
What new information are you observing? e.g. "200K downloads in 7 days" or "model flagged this review as spam".
4
Identify the Likelihoods — P(Evidence | State)
How probable is this evidence within each world state? Must come from historical data — never invent these. If not given, say: "I'd need historical data to estimate P(E|Hit) and P(E|Not Hit) accurately."
5
Compute the Posterior — P(State | Evidence)
Use Bayes' theorem. Calculate the denominator P(Evidence) first, then divide. This is your updated belief.
6
Make a Decision and Recommend
Don't just report the number. Say what you'd do with it. e.g. "Given a 48% posterior, I'd route flagged reviews to human verification rather than auto-deleting." State what data you'd collect next.

The Formula

P(State | Evidence) = P(Evidence | State) × P(State) ───────────────────────────────── P(Evidence) where: P(Evidence) = P(E | True) × P(True) + P(E | False) × P(False) In plain English: Posterior = Likelihood × Prior / Evidence

Sensitivity vs Specificity — Always Confusing

Sensitivity
P(Positive | Disease)
How well the test catches TRUE positives. If 90% sensitive → 90% of sick people test positive.
Specificity
P(Negative | No Disease)
How well the test rejects FALSE positives. If 95% specific → 95% of healthy people test negative.
False Positive Rate
1 − Specificity = P(Positive | No Disease)
How often healthy people are wrongly flagged. If 95% specific → FPR = 5%.
False Negative Rate
1 − Sensitivity = P(Negative | Disease)
How often sick people are wrongly cleared. If 90% sensitive → FNR = 10%.
Critical Rule
P(Evidence | True) and P(Evidence | False) do NOT need to sum to 1. They are probabilities within two different populations. What sums to 1: P(Positive | Disease) + P(Negative | Disease) = 1.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Easy
Disease Testing
2% of the population has a disease. A test has 95% sensitivity and 90% specificity. A person tests positive. What's the probability they actually have the disease?
Prior: P(Disease) = 0.02, P(No Disease) = 0.98 Likelihoods: P(Positive | Disease) = 0.95 ← sensitivity P(Positive | No Disease) = 0.10 ← 1 - specificity Denominator: P(Positive) = (0.95 × 0.02) + (0.10 × 0.98) = 0.019 + 0.098 = 0.117 Posterior: P(Disease | Positive) = 0.019 / 0.117 ≈ 16.2%
Business Insight
Despite testing positive, only 16.2% chance of actually having the disease. The low prior (2%) dominates. The doctor should recommend a confirmatory second test before diagnosis — never act on a single positive alone when the base rate is this low.
Example 2 — Medium
Email Spam Filter
1% of emails are spam. The spam filter has 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity. An email is flagged as spam. What's the probability it actually is spam?
Prior: P(Spam) = 0.01, P(Not Spam) = 0.99 Likelihoods: P(Flagged | Spam) = 0.99 ← sensitivity P(Flagged | Not Spam) = 0.01 ← 1 - specificity Denominator: P(Flagged) = (0.99 × 0.01) + (0.01 × 0.99) = 0.0099 + 0.0099 = 0.0198 Posterior: P(Spam | Flagged) = 0.0099 / 0.0198 = 50.0%
Business Insight
Even with a 99% accurate filter, a flagged email is only 50% likely to actually be spam. Moving flagged emails to trash would delete 1 in 2 legitimate emails. Route to a spam folder where users can review — don't auto-delete.
Example 3 — Hard (Google Play)
Google Play Review Spam Detection
5% of Google Play reviews are spam. Your spam detection model has 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity. A review is flagged as spam. What's the probability it actually is spam?
Prior: P(Spam) = 0.05, P(Not Spam) = 0.95 Likelihoods: P(Flagged | Spam) = 0.90 ← sensitivity P(Flagged | Not Spam) = 0.05 ← 1 - specificity Denominator: P(Flagged) = (0.90 × 0.05) + (0.05 × 0.95) = 0.045 + 0.0475 = 0.0925 Posterior: P(Spam | Flagged) = 0.045 / 0.0925 ≈ 48.65%
Business Insight
Barely better than a coin flip — even with a good model. I would not auto-delete flagged reviews. Instead, route to a human review queue or raise the model's decision threshold to improve precision at the cost of some recall.

The Base Rate Fallacy — Key Interview Insight

Even highly accurate models produce unreliable results when the event is rare. The prior dominates when the base rate is low. Always sanity-check your posterior against your prior — rare events require overwhelming evidence to shift the posterior significantly.

Prior = 2% (very rare)
16%
Posterior even with a 95%/90% test. Low prior dominates.
Prior = 5% (rare)
49%
Posterior with 90%/95% model. Still a coin flip!
Prior = 50% (common)
95%+
High prior → high posterior. Prior does the heavy lifting.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Making up likelihoods
Never invent P(Evidence | State) without data. If not given, say: "I'd need historical data to estimate this likelihood. Can you share what % of past hit games reached 200K in 7 days?" If asked to assume, state it explicitly and move on.
P(E|True) + P(E|False) = 1 is WRONG
These are probabilities within two different populations. They do not need to sum to 1. What does sum to 1: P(E|True) + P(not E|True) = 1.
Ignoring the prior (Base Rate Fallacy)
Never jump to conclusions from a model flag alone. A 90% accurate model flagging a rare event is far less reliable than it sounds. Always incorporate the prior.
Confusing sensitivity with specificity
Sensitivity → P(Positive | Disease). Specificity → P(Negative | No Disease). The false positive rate you plug into the formula is 1 − Specificity, not 1 − Sensitivity.
Not making a recommendation
Google always wants a decision. Don't just compute a number — say what you'd do with it, what guardrails you'd set, and what additional data you'd collect to refine your estimate.
