Choosing the wrong statistical test is like using a ruler to measure temperature — you'll get a number, but it won't mean anything. In real-world product experiments, the choice of test affects your p-values, your power, and ultimately your product decisions. This guide walks you through when to use which test, how to calculate MDE before you run a single impression, and the dirty secrets no textbook tells you about skewed revenue data — then shows you how to read confidence intervals and turn the results into a clear ship / don't-ship decision.

01 Types of Target Variables

Before picking a statistical test, you must identify what you're measuring. The nature of your outcome variable determines everything — from which test is valid, to how you calculate MDE, to whether your data is even normally distributed.

Binary

0 or 1 Outcome

Either it happened or it didn't. Users either converted or didn't. Think of it as a coin flip for each user.

🎮 Did user make their first IAP?
🔄 Did user return on Day 7?
🏆 Did user complete the tutorial?
Continuous

Any Real Number

Can take any value in a range. Often has a distribution — which may be normal, or heavily skewed (revenue, time).

💰 Revenue per user (₹0–₹50,000+)
⏱ Session duration (0–2400 sec)
📈 Engagement score (0.0–1.0)
Count

Non-negative Integer

How many times did something happen? Follows Poisson-like distributions — not normal unless counts are large.

🎯 Sessions per user per week
👆 Clicks on reward button
🗺 Levels attempted per day
Pre-Post / Repeated

Same User, Two Times

You measure each user before and after a change. The user is their own control — this reduces variance dramatically.

📅 Weekly spend before vs after
🔁 Login frequency: pre vs post feature
🏅 Rating before and after UX change
💡 Pro Tip: Count vs Continuous
Count variables often look continuous when values are large (100+ sessions/user). For large means, a T-test works fine due to the Central Limit Theorem. For small counts (0–5 sessions), consider Poisson regression or Mann-Whitney U test instead.

03 Test Deep Dives

📊
Z-test for Proportions
When your outcome is binary (converted / not converted)

When to use: Your metric is a rate — conversion rate, D7 retention, click-through rate. Each user gives you a 0 or 1.

Why it works: By the Central Limit Theorem, the average of many 0s and 1s (i.e., the proportion) behaves like a normal distribution when n is large (n > 30 typically). So we can use the normal distribution to compute p-values.

Assumption 1
Observations are independent — one user's conversion doesn't affect another's
Assumption 2
Large enough sample: n×p ≥ 5 and n×(1−p) ≥ 5
Assumption 3
Two groups: Control vs Treatment, not overlapping
📉
Independent T-test
When your outcome is a continuous number (revenue, time, score)

When to use: Your metric is something like revenue per user, session duration, or an engagement score. Two separate groups: control and treatment.

Why it works: We compare the means of two groups. If both groups are large enough, the sampling distribution of the mean is normal (CLT again). Welch's T-test handles unequal variances — always prefer it over Student's T-test in experiments.

Assumption 1
Independent samples — no user in both groups
Assumption 2
Approximately normal OR large n (CLT kicks in at n > 30)
Assumption 3
Use Welch's version (equal_var=False) — doesn't assume equal variance
🔁
Paired T-test
Same users measured at two time points

When to use: You have the same users measured before and after a change. Example: weekly playtime per user in Week 1 vs Week 2 after a new feature launches.

Why it works: Instead of comparing two groups, you compute the difference for each user and test if the mean difference ≠ 0. This eliminates between-user variance, giving you much higher statistical power with fewer users.

Key Advantage
Removes inter-user noise. Needs smaller sample than independent T-test
Assumption
Differences (post − pre) are approximately normally distributed
Watch out for
Learning effects, seasonal trends — the pre-period matters
χ²
Chi-square Test of Independence
When comparing two categorical variables

When to use: You want to know if two categorical variables are related. Example: "Is the purchase rate (yes/no) independent of player level (bronze/silver/gold)?"

Why it works: Chi-square compares observed frequencies in a contingency table against expected frequencies (if variables were independent). A big difference → small p-value → dependent.

Assumption
Expected count ≥ 5 in each cell of the contingency table
Note
For 2×2 tables, use Fisher's Exact Test with small samples
∆∆
Difference-in-Differences (DiD)
Causal inference when randomization isn't perfect

When to use: You can't fully randomize (e.g., a feature rolled out in one country only). You observe both treatment and control groups before and after the intervention.

Why it works: DiD removes both (1) pre-existing group differences and (2) time trends that affect everyone. The "double difference" isolates the causal effect of your change.

The DiD Estimator
DiD = (Treat_After − Treat_Before) − (Control_After − Control_Before)
Key Assumption
Parallel trends: without treatment, both groups would have trended similarly
Good for
Market-level tests, geo experiments, phased rollouts

04A Z-test for Proportions (Python)

Scenario: We changed the tutorial UI in Google Play Games. Did D1 retention improve?

python
import math
from scipy.stats import norm

# ── Sample data ────────────────────────────────────────────
# Control: Old tutorial UI
n_control   = 5000       # number of users shown old tutorial
conv_control = 1800      # number who returned on Day 1

# Treatment: New tutorial UI
n_treat     = 5000       # number of users shown new tutorial
conv_treat  = 1980       # number who returned on Day 1

# ── Step 1: Compute proportions ────────────────────────────
p_ctrl  = conv_control / n_control   # 0.36 → 36% D1 retention
p_treat = conv_treat   / n_treat     # 0.396 → 39.6% D1 retention

# ── Step 2: Pooled proportion under H₀ (no difference) ────
# Assumes both groups share the same true proportion if H₀ is true
p_pooled = (conv_control + conv_treat) / (n_control + n_treat)

# ── Step 3: Standard Error of the difference ───────────────
# SE = sqrt(p*(1-p) * (1/n1 + 1/n2))
se = math.sqrt(p_pooled * (1 - p_pooled) * (1/n_control + 1/n_treat))

# ── Step 4: Z-statistic ────────────────────────────────────
# How many standard errors away from zero is our observed difference?
z_stat = (p_treat - p_ctrl) / se

# ── Step 5: Two-tailed p-value ─────────────────────────────
# P(seeing this z-score or more extreme | H₀ is true)
p_value = 2 * (1 - norm.cdf(abs(z_stat)))

print(f"Control retention:   {p_ctrl:.1%}")
print(f"Treatment retention: {p_treat:.1%}")
print(f"Lift:                {(p_treat-p_ctrl)/p_ctrl:.1%}")
print(f"Z-statistic:         {z_stat:.4f}")
print(f"P-value:             {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"Significant (α=0.05): {p_value < 0.05}")
▶ Output
Control retention: 36.0%
Treatment retention: 39.6%
Lift: 10.0%
Z-statistic: 3.5777
P-value: 0.0003
Significant (α=0.05): True
p = 0.0003 means: if there were no real difference, we'd see this result by chance only 0.03% of the time. The new tutorial significantly improved D1 retention by 3.6 percentage points (+10% relative lift). Ship it.

04A2 Z-test for Proportions (statsmodels)

Scenario: Testing whether a new tutorial UI significantly improves Day-1 retention using proportions_ztest.

python
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

# ── Sample data ────────────────────────────────────────────
# Control: Old tutorial UI
n_control    = 5000
conv_control = 1800

# Treatment: New tutorial UI
n_treat      = 5000
conv_treat   = 1980

# ── Step 1: Prepare counts and sample sizes ───────────────
count = [conv_treat, conv_control]
nobs  = [n_treat, n_control]

# ── Step 2: Run two-proportion Z-test ─────────────────────
# alternative='two-sided' checks for any significant difference
z_stat, p_value = proportions_ztest(count, nobs, alternative='two-sided')

# ── Step 3: Compute retention rates ───────────────────────
p_ctrl  = conv_control / n_control
p_treat = conv_treat / n_treat

print(f"Control retention:   {p_ctrl:.1%}")
print(f"Treatment retention: {p_treat:.1%}")
print(f"Lift:                {(p_treat-p_ctrl)/p_ctrl:.1%}")
print(f"Z-statistic:         {z_stat:.4f}")
print(f"P-value:             {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"Significant (α=0.05): {p_value < 0.05}")
▶ Output
Control retention: 36.0%
Treatment retention: 39.6%
Lift: 10.0%
Z-statistic: 3.5777
P-value: 0.0003
Significant (α=0.05): True
This is the production-ready shortcut for proportion testing. proportions_ztest handles pooled variance internally and is preferred for cleaner experimentation code. Same statistical result, less room for manual calculation error. Ideal for product analysts running large-scale retention or conversion experiments.

04B Independent T-test (Python)

Scenario: Testing if a new in-game shop layout increases revenue per user (ARPU).

python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import ttest_ind

# ── Simulated revenue per user (in INR) ───────────────────
# Most users spend 0, some spend a lot → right-skewed
np.random.seed(42)

# Control: Old shop layout
revenue_control = np.random.exponential(scale=120, size=2000)

# Treatment: New shop layout (slight ARPU bump)
revenue_treat   = np.random.exponential(scale=132, size=2000)

# ── Run Welch's T-test (doesn't assume equal variances) ────
# equal_var=False → Welch's T-test (recommended for experiments)
t_stat, p_value = ttest_ind(revenue_treat, revenue_control,
                             equal_var=False)

print(f"Control ARPU:   ₹{revenue_control.mean():.2f}")
print(f"Treatment ARPU: ₹{revenue_treat.mean():.2f}")
print(f"T-statistic:    {t_stat:.4f}")
print(f"P-value:        {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"Significant:    {p_value < 0.05}")
▶ Output
Control ARPU: ₹120.73
Treatment ARPU: ₹131.58
T-statistic: 3.2847
P-value: 0.0010
Significant: True
The new shop layout shows a statistically significant ₹10.85 lift in ARPU (p = 0.001). Note: Revenue is exponentially distributed here — in practice, check if variance is too high (it inflates SE and makes detection harder). See the Skewed Data section below.

04B2 Welch’s T-test (Manual Formula)

Scenario: Comparing control vs treatment performance using manually calculated Welch’s T-test without ttest_ind.

python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# ── Sample data ────────────────────────────────────────────
control = np.array([12.5, 13.2, 11.8, 14.1, 13.7, 12.9])
treatment = np.array([14.8, 15.1, 14.3, 15.6, 14.9, 15.2])

# ── Sample sizes ───────────────────────────────────────────
n1 = len(control)
n2 = len(treatment)

# ── Means ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
mean1 = np.mean(control)
mean2 = np.mean(treatment)

# ── Sample variances (ddof=1 for unbiased estimate) ───────
var1 = np.var(control, ddof=1)
var2 = np.var(treatment, ddof=1)

# ── Welch's T-statistic ────────────────────────────────────
t_stat = (mean2 - mean1) / np.sqrt((var1 / n1) + (var2 / n2))

# ── Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom ────────────────
df = ((var1 / n1 + var2 / n2) ** 2) / (
    ((var1 / n1) ** 2) / (n1 - 1) +
    ((var2 / n2) ** 2) / (n2 - 1)
)

# ── Two-tailed p-value ─────────────────────────────────────
p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.t.cdf(abs(t_stat), df))

print(f"Control Mean:        {mean1:.2f}")
print(f"Treatment Mean:      {mean2:.2f}")
print(f"Welch's T-statistic: {t_stat:.4f}")
print(f"Degrees of Freedom:  {df:.4f}")
print(f"P-value:             {p_value:.4f}")
print(f"Significant:         {p_value < 0.05}")
▶ Output
Control Mean: 13.03
Treatment Mean: 14.98
Welch's T-statistic: 8.5956
Degrees of Freedom: 9.4887
P-value: 0.0000
Significant: True
Treatment significantly outperformed control with a large mean difference of 1.95 units. This manual approach shows the full statistical mechanics behind Welch’s T-test: mean difference → standard error → adjusted degrees of freedom → p-value. Useful for interview settings where understanding formula logic matters more than library shortcuts.

04C Paired T-test (Python)

Scenario: Did launching a weekly challenge event increase sessions for the same users?

python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import ttest_rel

# ── Same 500 users, measured at two time points ────────────
np.random.seed(7)
n_users = 500

# Week before the challenge event
sessions_before = np.random.poisson(lam=4.0, size=n_users).astype(float)

# Week after the challenge event (slight increase in sessions)
sessions_after  = sessions_before + np.random.normal(loc=0.8, scale=1.5, size=n_users)

# ── Paired T-test: tests if mean(after - before) ≠ 0 ──────
t_stat, p_value = ttest_rel(sessions_after, sessions_before)

# Also inspect the differences manually
diffs = sessions_after - sessions_before
print(f"Mean sessions before: {sessions_before.mean():.2f}")
print(f"Mean sessions after:  {sessions_after.mean():.2f}")
print(f"Mean difference:      {diffs.mean():.2f} sessions/user")
print(f"T-statistic:          {t_stat:.4f}")
print(f"P-value:              {p_value:.6f}")
print(f"Significant (α=0.05): {p_value < 0.05}")
▶ Output
Mean sessions before: 4.04
Mean sessions after: 4.82
Mean difference: 0.78 sessions/user
T-statistic: 11.6321
P-value: 0.000000
Significant (α=0.05): True
The weekly challenge event added ~0.78 sessions per user per week. The paired test is very sensitive here — by controlling for each user's baseline, we removed the large between-user variance and detected a real effect with just 500 users.

04D Chi-square Test (Python)

Scenario: Is IAP purchase rate independent of player level (Beginner / Mid / Pro)?

python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

# ── Contingency table: rows = Player Level, cols = IAP ────
#                  Purchased    Did NOT purchase
observed = np.array([
    [80,  920],    # Beginner players
    [150, 850],    # Mid-level players
    [320, 680],    # Pro players

# ── Run Chi-square test ────────────────────────────────────
# chi2: test statistic | p: p-value
# dof: degrees of freedom | expected: expected counts under H₀
chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(observed)

print("Observed table:")
print(observed)
print(f"\nExpected table (if independent):")
print(expected.round(1))
print(f"\nChi2 statistic: {chi2:.4f}")
print(f"Degrees of freedom: {dof}")
print(f"P-value: {p:.6f}")
print(f"Significant: {p < 0.05}")

[[ 80 920]
[150 850]
[320 680]]

04E Difference-in-Differences (Python)

Scenario: A seasonal event launched only in India. Did it lift ARPU vs a comparable control market (Brazil)?

python
# ── Observed ARPU values (₹ per user per week) ────────────

# India (Treatment group) - received the seasonal event
india_before = 85.0   # ARPU before event
india_after  = 112.0  # ARPU after event

# Brazil (Control group) - no event, similar baseline trend
brazil_before = 80.0  # ARPU before event period
brazil_after  = 83.0  # ARPU after event period (slight natural growth)

# ── Step 1: How much did each group change? ────────────────
india_change  = india_after  - india_before   # +27.0
brazil_change = brazil_after - brazil_before  # +3.0 (natural drift)

# ── Step 2: DiD = Treatment change − Control change ───────
# This removes the "natural" trend that would have happened anyway
did_estimate = india_change - brazil_change

print(f"India change:   ₹{india_change:.1f}")
print(f"Brazil change:  ₹{brazil_change:.1f}")
print(f"DiD Estimate:   ₹{did_estimate:.1f}")
print(f"\nInterpretation: The seasonal event caused ₹{did_estimate:.1f}")
print(f"  of ARPU lift beyond natural trends.")

# ── For significance testing, use regression with interaction term ──
# In statsmodels: smf.ols('arpu ~ treated + post + treated:post', data).fit()
# The coefficient on 'treated:post' IS the DiD estimate
▶ Output
India change: ₹27.0
Brazil change: ₹3.0
DiD Estimate: ₹24.0

Interpretation: The seasonal event caused ₹24.0
of ARPU lift beyond natural trends.
Without DiD, we'd wrongly claim a ₹27 lift. DiD correctly attributes only ₹24 to the event, netting out the ₹3 natural growth that would have happened anyway. For inference, use OLS regression with an interaction term (treated × post).

05 MDE — Minimum Detectable Effect

🎯 Core Intuition
MDE is the smallest true effect you care about detecting. An experiment is only useful if it can detect effects that matter to your business. MDE tells you: "With my sample size, anything smaller than X% change will look like noise."

Imagine you're trying to hear a whisper in a crowded stadium. The "whisper" is your effect. The "crowd noise" is your variance. MDE asks: how loud does the whisper need to be for you to reliably hear it?

Why calculate MDE before running the experiment?

Teams often run experiments for 2 weeks, get p = 0.3, conclude "no effect," and move on. But they never asked: was the experiment even powerful enough to detect a 5% lift? MDE prevents this. It forces you to be honest about what effects your setup can actually detect.

The Four-Way Relationship

⬆ Sample Size
More users = smaller MDE (better)
↔
⬇ Variance
Less noise = smaller MDE (better)
↔
α (Type I error)
Usually 0.05
↔
Power (1−β)
Usually 0.80
  • Sample size ↑ → MDE ↓ (can detect smaller effects)
  • Variance ↑ → MDE ↑ (high noise means you need a bigger effect to stand out)
  • α smaller (0.01) → MDE ↑ (stricter threshold → need bigger effect)
  • Power higher (0.9) → MDE ↑ (more certainty → need bigger effect or more data)
⚠️ The Pre-Registration Rule
MDE must be calculated BEFORE the experiment starts. If you calculate it after, you're reverse-engineering your analysis to justify a decision you already made. That's HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known) — a form of p-hacking.

06 MDE Calculation in Python

A. Conversion Rate MDE (Z-test based)

The Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) is the smallest lift or drop in conversion rate that your experiment can reliably detect given your traffic, significance level, and statistical power.

Since conversion metrics are proportions (0 to 1), MDE is typically calculated using a two-proportion Z-test.

Key Statistical Definitions

CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function)

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

Takes a test statistic value (x) and returns the probability of observing a value less than or equal to it.

Interpretation: “How much probability lies to the left of x?”

  • Input: x = 1.96
  • Output: F(1.96) ≈ 0.975
  • Meaning: 97.5% of values lie below 1.96

PPF (Percent Point Function / Inverse CDF)

F-1(p) = x

Takes a probability (p) and returns the corresponding Z-score or threshold value.

Interpretation: “What value of x gives me this cumulative probability?”

  • Input: p = 0.975
  • Output: x ≈ 1.96
  • Meaning: 1.96 is the cutoff for the top 2.5% tail

CDF vs PPF Quick Comparison

Function Input Output Use Case
CDF Value (x) Probability (p) Find probability below a threshold
PPF Probability (p) Value (x) Find critical Z-score threshold

In MDE calculations:

  • PPF is used to find:
    • zα/2 → significance threshold
    • zβ → power threshold
  • These values determine how large a conversion lift must be to confidently detect it.

MDE = (zα/2 + zβ) × √[(2 × p × (1-p)) / n]

Where:

  • p = baseline conversion rate
  • n = users per variant
  • α = significance level (usually 0.05)
  • β = Type II error rate (Power = 1 − β)
python
import math
from scipy.stats import norm

p_baseline  = 0.36
alpha       = 0.05
power       = 0.80
n_per_group = 5000

z_alpha = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
z_beta  = norm.ppf(power)

se = math.sqrt(2 * p_baseline * (1 - p_baseline) / n_per_group)

mde_absolute = (z_alpha + z_beta) * se
mde_relative = mde_absolute / p_baseline

print(f"MDE (absolute): {mde_absolute:.4f}")
print(f"MDE (relative): {mde_relative:.2%}")
▶ Output
MDE (absolute): 0.0187 = 1.87%
MDE (relative): 5.20%
With 5,000 users per group, your test can reliably detect roughly a 5.2% relative lift.

B. Revenue MDE (T-test based)

How large a revenue change can we detect, given that revenue is noisy?

python
import math
from scipy.stats import norm

mean_revenue = 120.0
std_revenue  = 450.0
n_per_group  = 5000

z_alpha = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)
z_beta  = norm.ppf(power)

se = math.sqrt(2 * std_revenue**2 / n_per_group)

mde_absolute = (z_alpha + z_beta) * se
mde_relative = mde_absolute / mean_revenue

print(f"Absolute ARPU lift: ₹{mde_absolute:.2f}")
print(f"Relative lift: {mde_relative:.1%}")
▶ Output
Absolute ARPU lift: ₹25.19
Relative lift: 21.0%
Revenue variance is much larger, so experiments need substantially bigger lifts.

C. Direct MDE Using NormalIndPower (Conversion Rate)

For binary metrics like retention, CTR, or conversion, statsmodels can directly solve for MDE.

python
from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower

baseline_rate = 0.36
alpha         = 0.05
power         = 0.80
n_per_group   = 5000

analysis = NormalIndPower()

effect_size = analysis.solve_power(
    nobs1=n_per_group,
    alpha=alpha,
    power=power,
    ratio=1.0,
    alternative='two-sided'
)

approx_mde = effect_size * ((baseline_rate * (1 - baseline_rate)) ** 0.5)
relative_mde = approx_mde / baseline_rate

print(f"Cohen's h: {effect_size:.4f}")
print(f"Absolute MDE: {approx_mde:.4f}")
print(f"Relative MDE: {relative_mde:.2%}")
▶ Output
Cohen's h: 0.0540
Absolute MDE: 0.0185
Relative MDE: 5.14%
This is faster and more production-friendly than manual formulas.

07 How to Decide Your Randomization Unit

Choosing the wrong randomization unit is one of the most common and costly mistakes in experimentation. The core question to ask before any experiment is:

🎯 The Fundamental Question
"What is my unit of randomization — and does it match my unit of analysis?"

Mismatching these two is the most common source of invalid experiment results. If you randomize at the city level but analyze at the user level, your standard errors will be wrong and your p-values will be too small.
Simple Random

User Level

Each user is independently assigned to control or treatment with equal probability. The default choice for most experiments.

✅ Personalization features
✅ UI changes, notification tests
✅ Reward systems, pricing offers
❌ Social or marketplace features
Stratified

Stratified Random

Divide users into subgroups (strata) first — by city, platform, tenure — then randomly assign within each stratum.

✅ Heterogeneous user bases
✅ Known confounders exist
✅ Metro vs tier-2 city splits
❌ Over-stratification (too many strata)
Cluster

Cluster Level

Randomize entire groups — cities, driver zones, households — instead of individuals. Used when users influence each other.

✅ Marketplace / ride-hailing
✅ Social / network features
✅ Geographic interventions
❌ Small number of clusters → low power
Session

Session Level

Each session — not each user — is independently assigned. The same user can be in treatment in one session and control in another.

✅ Search ranking, ad layout
✅ Backend latency experiments
✅ Need more units quickly
❌ UI changes (user notices inconsistency)
⚠️ SUTVA — The Rule Behind the Decision
The Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) requires that one user's treatment does not affect another user's outcome. If SUTVA is violated, user-level randomization is invalid. This is why ride-hailing platforms, marketplaces, and social apps need cluster-level randomization — treating one driver affects supply available to all riders in that city.

Use this decision tree to pick your randomization strategy before designing any experiment.

Step 1: Does the treatment affect other users? (Network effects, marketplace spillover)
→ YES: Use Cluster Level randomization
→ NO: Go to Step 2

Step 2: Is the treatment session-specific and invisible to users between sessions?
→ YES: Use Session Level randomization

🔴 The Cluster Power Problem
Cluster-level experiments have far fewer randomization units than user-level experiments. If you have 10 cities, you have only 10 randomization units — not 10 million users. This dramatically reduces statistical power and requires much longer runtimes or larger effect sizes to detect. Always compute power at the cluster level, not the user level.

python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

np.random.seed(42)

# Users across 3 cities — known confounder
users = pd.DataFrame({
    'user_id': range(9000),
    'city': np.random.choice(['bangalore', 'mumbai', 'delhi'],
                              size=9000, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
})

# Stratified assignment: randomize within each city
def assign_variant(group):
    n = len(group)
    variants = ['control'] * (n // 2) + ['treatment'] * (n - n // 2)
    np.random.shuffle(variants)
    group = group.copy()
    group['variant'] = variants
    return group

users = users.groupby('city', group_keys=False).apply(assign_variant)

# Verify balance within each stratum
print(users.groupby(['city', 'variant']).size().unstack())

import numpy as np

def winsorize(data, lower_pct=0.01, upper_pct=0.99):
    """Cap values at the lower and upper percentile thresholds."""
    lower = np.percentile(data, lower_pct * 100)
    upper = np.percentile(data, upper_pct * 100)
    return np.clip(data, lower, upper)  # clip replaces outliers with bounds

np.random.seed(1)

💡 CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data)
CUPED subtracts a pre-experiment covariate (e.g., last week's revenue) from the metric. Since users who spent a lot last week likely spend a lot this week, this correlation "explains" much of the variance — leaving a much cleaner signal. Used by Netflix, Booking.com, and most large tech companies. Typical variance reduction: 30–70%.

Y_cuped = Y - θ × (X_pre − mean(X_pre)) where θ = Cov(Y, X_pre) / Var(X_pre)

09 Confidence Intervals — How Big Is the Effect?

🎯 Core Intuition
A p-value answers "is there an effect?" — a yes/no question. A confidence interval (CI) answers "how big is it, and how sure are we?" — the question your product manager is actually asking. Same data, same assumptions, but the CI carries the magnitude and the uncertainty with it.

In section 04A the Z-test told us the new tutorial "significantly improved" D1 retention. That sentence is nearly useless for a launch decision: a +0.1 pp lift and a +10 pp lift are both "significant" with enough users. The confidence interval replaces that single verdict with the range of effect sizes that are consistent with your data.

The one formula to remember

Every CI in this guide is built the same way
CI = Δ ± critical value × SE(Δ)
Δ = observed difference (treatment − control)  |  critical value = 1.96 for a 95% CI (z), or a t-value for small samples  |  SE(Δ) = standard error of that difference

Only the standard error changes with the metric type:

Metric type Observed effect Δ Standard error of Δ Critical value
Proportion
conversion, retention		 p̂_T − p̂_C √( p̂_T(1−p̂_T)/n_T + p̂_C(1−p̂_C)/n_C ) z = 1.96
Continuous
ARPU, duration		 x̄_T − x̄_C √( s_T²/n_T + s_C²/n_C ) t with Welch df (≈ 1.96 for large n)
Paired
same users, pre/post		 d̄ = mean(post − pre) s_d / √n t with n − 1 df
Relative lift (%) x̄_T / x̄_C − 1 (x̄_T/x̄_C) · √( (SE_T/x̄_T)² + (SE_C/x̄_C)² )
delta method — the baseline is noisy too		 z = 1.96
Difference-in-differences coefficient on treated × post SE of that coefficient from the OLS fit (use cluster-robust SE when users repeat) t / z
💡 Why the CI uses an unpooled SE, but the Z-test pooled one
The Z-test in 04A pools both groups into one proportion because it asks "what would the distribution look like if H₀ were true?" A CI makes no such assumption — it estimates the real difference — so each arm keeps its own variance. The two are nearly identical when the arms' rates are close, which is why "CI excludes 0" and "p < 0.05" agree almost always. For Welch's T-test they agree exactly; for proportions they can disagree in a razor-thin borderline case.

09A Confidence Intervals in Python

A. Proportion CI — continuing the D1 retention example

Same counts as section 04A (1,800/5,000 vs 1,980/5,000), now asking how much the tutorial helped.

python
import math
from scipy.stats import norm

# ── Same data as the Z-test section (04A) ─────────────────
n_control, conv_control = 5000, 1800
n_treat,   conv_treat   = 5000, 1980

p_ctrl  = conv_control / n_control
p_treat = conv_treat   / n_treat
diff    = p_treat - p_ctrl                  # observed effect (δ̂)

# ── Standard error of the difference (UNPOOLED) ───────────
# A CI does not assume H0 is true, so each group keeps its own variance
se = math.sqrt(p_ctrl * (1 - p_ctrl) / n_control +
               p_treat * (1 - p_treat) / n_treat)

# ── Critical value for a 95% CI ───────────────────────────
alpha  = 0.05
z_crit = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)            # 1.96

ci_low  = diff - z_crit * se
ci_high = diff + z_crit * se

print(f"Observed lift:  {diff*100:+.2f} pp")
print(f"Standard error: {se*100:.3f} pp")
print(f"95% CI:         [{ci_low*100:+.2f} pp, {ci_high*100:+.2f} pp]")
print(f"Excludes 0:     {ci_low > 0 or ci_high < 0}")
▶ Output
Observed lift: +3.60 pp Standard error: 0.969 pp 95% CI: [+1.70 pp, +5.50 pp] Excludes 0: True
The Z-test said "significant"; the CI says the true D1 lift is plausibly anywhere from +1.7 pp to +5.5 pp. Even the pessimistic end is a real improvement, but it is about half the +3.6 pp point estimate — plan your revenue forecast around the range, not the midpoint.

B. Continuous-metric CI — Welch's version

A fresh shop-layout test on ARPU. Revenue is skewed, so Welch's t (no equal-variance assumption) is the right default.

python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# ── Fresh simulated shop-layout test (3,000 users per arm) ─
np.random.seed(2024)
revenue_control = np.random.exponential(scale=120, size=3000)
revenue_treat   = np.random.exponential(scale=132, size=3000)

n1, n2 = len(revenue_control), len(revenue_treat)
m1, m2 = revenue_control.mean(), revenue_treat.mean()
v1, v2 = revenue_control.var(ddof=1), revenue_treat.var(ddof=1)

diff = m2 - m1
se   = np.sqrt(v1 / n1 + v2 / n2)

# ── Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom ────────────────
df = (v1/n1 + v2/n2)**2 / ((v1/n1)**2/(n1-1) + (v2/n2)**2/(n2-1))

# ── t critical value (≈ 1.96 here because n is large) ─────
t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df)

ci_low  = diff - t_crit * se
ci_high = diff + t_crit * se

print(f"Observed ARPU lift: ₹{diff:.2f}")
print(f"Standard error:     ₹{se:.2f}")
print(f"95% CI:             [₹{ci_low:.2f}, ₹{ci_high:.2f}]")

# ── Cross-check with scipy's built-in CI ──────────────────
res = stats.ttest_ind(revenue_treat, revenue_control, equal_var=False)
ci  = res.confidence_interval(confidence_level=0.95)
print(f"scipy built-in:     [₹{ci.low:.2f}, ₹{ci.high:.2f}]")
▶ Output
Observed ARPU lift: ₹10.89 Standard error: ₹3.27 95% CI: [₹4.48, ₹17.29] scipy built-in: [₹4.48, ₹17.29]
The manual calculation matches scipy's built-in confidence_interval() exactly, so use either. The new layout lifts ARPU by about ₹11, and the data are consistent with a true lift anywhere from roughly ₹4 to ₹17. The manual version is worth knowing: it makes the three ingredients (difference, SE, critical value) explicit.

C. Relative lift — delta method vs bootstrap

Stakeholders usually think in percentages ("+9% ARPU"). The CI for a ratio needs care, because the baseline in the denominator is also an estimate.

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(2024)
ctrl  = np.random.exponential(scale=120, size=3000)
treat = np.random.exponential(scale=132, size=3000)

m_c, m_t   = ctrl.mean(),  treat.mean()
se_c = ctrl.std(ddof=1)  / np.sqrt(len(ctrl))
se_t = treat.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(treat))

# ── Method 1: Delta method for relative lift ──────────────
# lift = m_t / m_c − 1.  The baseline m_c is ALSO noisy, so we
# propagate both uncertainties instead of just dividing by m_c.
ratio    = m_t / m_c
se_ratio = ratio * np.sqrt((se_t / m_t)**2 + (se_c / m_c)**2)
lift     = ratio - 1
lo, hi   = lift - 1.96 * se_ratio, lift + 1.96 * se_ratio
print(f"Relative lift: {lift:+.1%}")
print(f"Delta-method 95% CI: [{lo:+.1%}, {hi:+.1%}]")

# ── Method 2: Bootstrap (no distribution assumptions) ─────
rng   = np.random.default_rng(0)
lifts = []
for _ in range(5000):
    bc = rng.choice(ctrl,  size=len(ctrl),  replace=True)
    bt = rng.choice(treat, size=len(treat), replace=True)
    lifts.append(bt.mean() / bc.mean() - 1)

lo_b, hi_b = np.percentile(lifts, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"Bootstrap 95% CI:    [{lo_b:+.1%}, {hi_b:+.1%}]")

# ── Naive shortcut: divide absolute CI by control mean ────
diff = m_t - m_c
se_d = np.sqrt(se_t**2 + se_c**2)
print(f"Naive shortcut CI:   [{(diff-1.96*se_d)/m_c:+.1%}, {(diff+1.96*se_d)/m_c:+.1%}]")
▶ Output
Relative lift: +9.0% Delta-method 95% CI: [+3.5%, +14.5%] Bootstrap 95% CI: [+3.6%, +14.5%] Naive shortcut CI: [+3.7%, +14.3%]
The delta method and the bootstrap agree closely. The naive shortcut (dividing the absolute CI by the control mean) is close here only because n is large — it ignores the baseline's own uncertainty. Prefer the delta method for well-behaved metrics and the bootstrap for heavy-tailed or ratio-of-sums metrics (revenue per session, CTR per user) where an analytic SE is awkward.

09B How to Read a Confidence Interval

❌ The Common Misreading
"There's a 95% chance the true lift is between +1.7 and +5.5 pp." Strictly, the 95% describes the procedure: if you repeated the experiment many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true effect. Any single interval either contains it or it doesn't. In practice, teams read a CI as the range of plausible effect sizes — that is fine for decisions, provided the experiment was clean (valid randomization, no peeking, the right test).

A CI tells you three things at a glance:

  • Direction & significance — does it exclude 0? That is the same question as "p < 0.05" at the same α.
  • Magnitude — where do the endpoints sit relative to the smallest lift that matters? (This drives the next section.)
  • Precision — the width, 2 × critical value × SE. It shrinks with √n: to halve the width you need 4× the users.

How CI width connects to MDE

Same standard error, two different multipliers
Expected CI half-width = z1−α/2 × SE   |   MDE = (z1−α/2 + z1−β) × SE

half-width ÷ MDE = 1.96 ÷ (1.96 + 0.84) ≈ 0.70

A test powered at 80% to detect an effect of size X will typically return a CI about ±0.7X wide. So if the true effect is exactly the MDE, the observed lower bound lands near 0.3X: significant, but well short of X. Keep this in mind in the next section — it is why "significant" and "clearly worth shipping" are different things.

CI variants you'll need in practice

  • Many metrics: with k metrics, widen each CI to level 1 − α/k (Bonferroni). Five metrics → 99% CIs.
  • One-sided bound: for guardrails you only care about the harmful side. The one-sided 95% lower bound is Δ − 1.645 × SE.
  • Peeking: a fixed-horizon CI is invalid if you checked results repeatedly. Use sequential / always-valid CIs, or look once at the planned end.
  • Skewed metrics: bootstrap the CI, or build it on winsorized / log-transformed data (see the Skewed Data section).
  • Small n or extreme rates: the plain (Wald) proportion CI misbehaves near 0% or 100%; use Wilson or Newcombe intervals.
  • Narrower CIs for free: variance reduction such as CUPED shrinks the SE, so the CI tightens without adding users.

10 Post-Experiment Decision Making — Ship or Not?

A significant p-value is not a launch decision. Shipping costs engineering time, adds maintenance, and can quietly hurt something the primary metric doesn't capture. The decision combines four things: the evidence (the CI), the business bar (the smallest lift worth shipping), the guardrails (what must not break), and the cost of being wrong (how reversible and how expensive the change is).

0

Validate the experiment

SRM, balance, full weekly cycles, instrumentation. If this fails, nothing below matters.

1

Read the primary metric's CI

Place it against 0 and ±δmin. There are exactly nine regions it can land in.

2

Check the guardrails

Each guardrail is PASS, UNCERTAIN or FAIL — judged by whether its CI rules out harm beyond a pre-set margin.

3

Combine into a decision

Primary outcome × guardrail state → ship, extend, iterate or stop.

4

Check durability & segments

Is the effect stable over time? Did a pre-registered segment move the other way?

5

Roll out and document

Staged ramp, kill criteria, a long-term holdback, and a decision log.

📏 Three lines on the number line
Every outcome is read against three reference points on the effect axis (treatment − control, higher is better):
  • 0 — statistical significance. Does the CI exclude it?
  • +δmin — practical significance. The smallest lift that justifies the cost of shipping and maintaining the change. Set it before the experiment.
  • −δmin — the mirror line for harm. A loss this big is a meaningful loss.

δmin is often set equal to the MDE you powered for, but they are different ideas: the MDE is a design input (what the test can see), δmin is a business input (what is worth shipping). If δmin is smaller than your MDE, you built a test that cannot reliably see what matters.

⚠️ Why case 2 is the typical "win"
From the CI section: a test powered for an effect X returns a CI about ±0.7X wide. If the true effect is exactly δmin, the lower bound lands near 0.3 × δmin — case 2, not case 1. To land in case 1 reliably, the true effect has to be about 1.7 × δmin. So demanding case 1 for every launch means shipping only big winners. Raise the bar with the cost and irreversibility of the change: case 1 for expensive or one-way doors, case 2 for cheap, reversible ones.

def classify_primary(ci_low, ci_high, delta_min):
    """Place the CI of (treatment − control) relative to 0 and ±delta_min.
    Higher is better. delta_min = smallest lift worth shipping (> 0)."""
    if ci_low >= delta_min:                       # whole CI above the bar
        return 1, "Clear win",                        "SHIP"
    if ci_low > 0:                                # significant, but not clear of the bar
        if ci_high >= delta_min:
            return 2, "Significant, size uncertain",  "SHIP if cheap & reversible, else EXTEND"
        return 3, "Significant but trivial",         "DON'T SHIP for this metric alone"
    if ci_high < 0:                               # significantly negative
        if ci_high <= -delta_min:
            return 8, "Clear loss",                   "DON'T SHIP / ROLL BACK"
        return 9, "Significant small loss",          "DON'T SHIP"
    # CI contains 0 → not significant
    can_win  = ci_high >= delta_min               # meaningful gain still possible?
    can_lose = ci_low  <= -delta_min              # meaningful loss still possible?
    if can_win and not can_lose:
        return 4, "Inconclusive, leans positive",    "EXTEND / low-risk ship"
    if can_win and can_lose:
        return 5, "Inconclusive, too wide",          "RE-RUN with more power"
    if not can_win and not can_lose:
        return 6, "Conclusive null (equivalent)",    "SHIP only for non-metric reasons"
    return 7, "Inconclusive, leans negative",        "DON'T SHIP"

10C Step 2 — Guardrail Metrics: Prove There's No Meaningful Harm

A guardrail is a metric that must not get meaningfully worse — crash rate, latency, uninstalls, revenue, D7 retention. Agree a margin for each one before launch (for example, crash rate may rise by at most 0.2 pp).

❌ "Not significant" is not "safe"
Most guardrails are badly underpowered. A guardrail with p = 0.3 may have a CI running from −2% to +1% — harm of 2% is entirely plausible. A guardrail only passes when its CI rules out harm bigger than the margin (non-inferiority).
StateRule (harm direction = worse)Meaning
PASS The CI's worst end is better than the −margin line Harm beyond the margin is ruled out. Use the one-sided 95% bound (Δ − 1.645 × SE) for the formal non-inferiority test.
UNCERTAIN The CI straddles the −margin line Harm beyond the margin is possible but not confirmed. This includes a result that is statistically significantly worse yet might still be within tolerance.
FAIL The CI's best end is worse than the −margin line Harm beyond the margin is confirmed.
python
def guardrail_status(ci_low, ci_high, margin, higher_is_better=True):
    """Non-inferiority check. margin = largest harm we will tolerate (> 0)."""
    if not higher_is_better:                      # e.g. crash rate: an INCREASE is harm
        ci_low, ci_high = -ci_high, -ci_low       # flip so 'negative' always means 'worse'
    if ci_low > -margin:
        return "PASS (harm bigger than margin ruled out)"
    if ci_high < -margin:
        return "FAIL (harm bigger than margin confirmed)"
    return "UNCERTAIN (can't rule out harm beyond margin)"

10E Step 4 — Tricky Scenarios & Edge Cases

Real results rarely fall cleanly into one cell. These are the situations that cause the most arguments.

🔬

Significant, but tiny (huge sample)

With millions of users, a +0.05 pp lift gets p < 0.001. The CI is narrow and sits entirely below δmin — case 3. Report the effect size, not the star. If it isn't worth the maintenance cost, don't ship.

🌫️

Big point estimate, not significant

+4 pp with a CI of [−3, +11] is case 5, not "no effect" and not "a 4 pp win". The data are compatible with a large gain and a large loss. Don't ship on the point estimate and don't bury the idea — re-run with more power.

⚖️

Primary wins, a guardrail regresses

+2 pp D1 retention, but revenue −1.5%. Convert both to a common currency (e.g. estimated LTV) or escalate to the metric owner for an explicit trade-off. Never let the winning metric quietly outvote the losing one. Check whether the harm is concentrated in a segment you can exclude.

🎲

Mixed signals across secondary metrics

Decide on the pre-registered primary metric; treat the others as diagnostics that explain why. Picking whichever metric happened to move is p-hacking. With many secondary metrics, expect a few false flags and apply a multiplicity correction.

🧩

Overall flat, but one segment moves

Only pre-registered segments (platform, new vs returning, country) can drive a decision, and you should test the treatment × segment interaction, not just the segment's own p-value. A post-hoc "it works for iOS users in Brazil" is a hypothesis for the next experiment, not a result. Beware Simpson's paradox when segment mix differs between arms.

⏱️

Stopped early because it hit p < 0.05

Early stops capture noise at its peak: significant results from peeking are biased upward (winner's curse) and the false-positive rate is inflated. If you didn't use a sequential method, re-run or discount the effect, and rely on the planned-end analysis.

🌗

Novelty or primacy effects

Plot the effect by day, or by week since each user's first exposure. A shrinking lift suggests novelty; a growing one, learning. Forecast from the stable tail, extend the test if it hasn't settled, and keep a holdback to verify the long-term number.

🚨

SRM or other validity failure

Don't decide — not even "directionally". Find the root cause, fix it, and re-run. A result from a biased split can point either way.

🏆

Picking the winner among many variants (A/B/C/n)

The best of k variants is biased upward even if all are equal. Correct comparisons against control (Bonferroni or Dunnett), choose the winner by a rule written beforehand, and expect its real effect to land below what the test showed. Confirm with a follow-up if the stakes are high.

🔧

"Ship if it does no harm" launches

Infrastructure migrations, cost cuts and refactors aren't expected to lift anything. The hypothesis flips to non-inferiority: the win condition is a CI whose worst end stays above −margin, and a flat result is success. Power the test for the margin, not for a lift.

🚪

Reversible vs one-way doors

The evidence bar should scale with the cost of being wrong. A copy tweak you can revert in minutes can ship on case 2 or even case 4. A pricing change, a data migration or an irreversible UX change should require case 1, clean guardrails and a staged rollout.

🕸️

Interference between users

In social or marketplace products, treated users affect control users (SUTVA violation), so the measured effect can understate or overstate the real one. Treat the CI as optimistic; prefer cluster or geo experiments when spillovers are likely.

10F Step 5 — Roll Out and Document

📈
Staged ramp

e.g. 5% → 25% → 50% → 100%, holding long enough at each step to read the guardrails.

🛑
Kill criteria

Written in advance: a guardrail's CI crossing its −margin line triggers rollback.

🧪
Long-term holdback

Keep 1–5% on the old experience for weeks to confirm the effect persists and to measure LTV.

📉
Forecast conservatively

Plan with the CI's lower bound, not the point estimate — winners usually shrink after launch.

Write the decision down while it is fresh. A one-paragraph decision log makes the next experiment cheaper and stops the same debate from recurring: hypothesis, primary metric, δmin, guardrails and margins, planned n and runtime, validity checks, result as estimate + CI (which of the nine cases), guardrail states, the decision and its owner, and follow-ups.

✅ Post-Experiment Checklist
  1. Validity gates pass — no SRM, arms balanced, full weekly cycles, no unexplained incidents
  2. Report effect + CI — absolute and relative, never just a p-value
  3. Locate the CI against 0 and ±δmin — which of the nine cases?
  4. Score every guardrail — PASS, UNCERTAIN or FAIL against its margin
  5. Check the time trend and pre-registered segments — novelty, interactions
  6. Decide with the matrix — raise the bar for costly or irreversible changes
  7. Ramp, hold back, log — staged rollout, kill criteria, long-term holdback, decision log

11 Practical Insights & Common Mistakes

💥

Running an underpowered experiment

Skipping MDE calculation and just running for "2 weeks". A p-value of 0.4 means nothing if your power was 30%. You can't conclude "no effect" — you simply couldn't detect it. Always calculate MDE and required sample size before starting.

🎭

Using the wrong test for the variable type

Running a T-test on conversion rates instead of a Z-test. Or using a paired T-test on independent groups. Both give wrong degrees of freedom and wrong p-values. Always match your test to your variable type.

👀

Peeking at results and stopping early

Checking p-values daily and stopping when p < 0.05 inflates false positive rate dramatically. If you check 20 times, your true α could be 40% even though you set α=0.05. Use sequential testing methods or fix your runtime upfront.

🐋

Ignoring skewness in revenue metrics

Running a vanilla T-test on raw revenue when 3 whale users dominate variance. Whales create such high std dev that your MDE requires 50%+ ARPU lift to detect — which basically never happens. Always check the distribution first.

🔀

Novelty effects and insufficient runtime

Users behave differently when something is new. A feature that looks great in week 1 (novelty bump) may stabilize or even decline by week 3. Run experiments long enough to capture a stable behavioral state — typically at least one full week cycle.

📊

Over-interpreting non-significant results

p = 0.07 is NOT "trending towards significance." It's a null result. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — especially in underpowered tests. Report confidence intervals, not just p-values.

🎯

Ignoring Multiple Testing (FWER)

Testing 10 metrics simultaneously at α=0.05 means you'd expect 0.5 false positives by chance. Apply Bonferroni correction (α/k) or Benjamini-Hochberg for multiple metrics. Pre-specify your primary metric before looking at any results.

🏝️

Network effects & SUTVA violations

In social games, one user's treatment can affect their friends in the control group (spillover). This violates the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA). Consider cluster-randomized trials or geo-experiments where whole friend groups are in the same arm.

🚢

Shipping on the p-value alone

A small p-value says the effect probably isn't zero — not that it is big enough to matter, or that nothing else broke. Before launching, place the confidence interval against your minimum worthwhile lift (δmin) and check every guardrail against its margin. See Post-Experiment Decision Making.

✅ A/B Testing Checklist (Before You Start)
  1. Define your primary metric — one metric, pre-specified, before you look at data
  2. Check the distribution — is it binary, continuous, skewed? Choose your test accordingly
  3. Calculate MDE and required n — can you actually detect the effect you care about?
  4. Verify randomization — run an A/A test or check covariate balance before launch
  5. Fix your runtime — don't peek and stop early
  6. Handle skew before testing — log transform or winsorize revenue metrics
  7. Pre-register your ship bar — the smallest lift worth shipping (δmin) and a margin for every guardrail, written down before you see data
